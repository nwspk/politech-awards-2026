## Part B: Value Modifiers

### M1: Boost — tools built for practitioners who have no existing alternatives
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** +8–12 points
- **Trigger:** The primary beneficiary is a practitioner or community group (journalists, musicians, local publishers, community organisers) who lack existing alternatives that are free, open, and genuinely accessible. The project addresses a real access gap, not a convenience improvement for already-resourced users.
- **Evidence:** teeline.online was built because professional shorthand learning tools either cost money or were inaccessible online. Soli was built because streaming platforms "fuck musicians." Dummy was built because community journalists could not afford corporate publishing infrastructure. Every Gonzo Engineering project starts from the same premise: someone is being priced out or locked out of something they need. [gonzo.engineering, teeline.online About, GitHub: soli, GitHub: dummy — CONFIRMED]

### M2: Reduce — extractive platform capture or surveillance-adjacent architecture
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** −8–12 points
- **Trigger:** The project relies on corporate platform APIs for its core function, centralises user data in ways that benefit the operator rather than the user, includes advertising or behavioural tracking, or is owned by or substantially dependent on a major tech corporation (Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, Apple). Also applies when the project's architecture makes users captive to a single provider — lock-in by design.
- **Evidence:** Gonzo Engineering's explicit mission is anti-extraction: "bypasses tech giants," IPFS instead of corporate streaming infrastructure, open source to prevent platform lock-in. A project that reproduces the structures he is building against would be strongly reduced. [GitHub: dummy README, GitHub: soli README, gonzo.engineering — CONFIRMED]

### M3: Boost — community ownership or governance — power to practitioners not institutions
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** +5–8 points
- **Trigger:** The project is governed by, accountable to, or meaningfully co-created with the communities it serves. Not just user-feedback loops but structural power: community ownership, open governance, cooperative models, community-controlled data, multi-stakeholder boards that include end users.
- **Evidence:** Social Streets CIC is a community interest company — governance structure that explicitly serves community rather than shareholders. teeline.online is explicitly built with user feedback and community contributions. The CIC model at Social Streets is significant: not-for-profit, community-serving, anti-extractive. [socialstreets.co, teeline.online About — CONFIRMED; Gonzo Engineering framing — PROBABLE]

### M4: Reduce — tools that automate civic judgment or replace human decision-making

- **Magnitude:** −5–8 points
- **Trigger:** The project's primary value proposition is replacing or significantly reducing human judgment in civic, political, or editorial processes — automated content generation, algorithmic scoring of citizens, automated summarisation replacing reading, AI-generated political communication at scale. Distinct from tools that augment human judgment (which do not trigger this modifier).
- **Evidence:** "Technology should serve keen minds, not replace them." [bio, GitHub: gonzo-engineering org description, teeline.online — CONFIRMED]

### M5: Boost — equitable economics for creative and media workers

| Rule | Statement | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Abstention threshold | A project receives N/A (abstention) only if: (a) the project URL returns a dead link AND there is no useful dossier text (less than 2 substantive fields populated beyond name/URL), OR (b) the project is so ambiguous from the dossier that none of the six criteria can be assessed even tentatively. | Prototype handling | Prototypes are protected from implementation maturity penalties. M6 (epistemic humility) may additionally boost genuinely exploratory projects. | Tie-breaking | When two projects score equally after criteria and modifiers: prefer the project that serves more excluded or less-resourced users. If still tied: prefer the project with greater transparency (open source, documented governance, accessible code). If still tied: prefer the project with a more explicit theory of change for how it benefits practitioners. | Two projects have equal final scores |
| Uncertainty handling | Uncertainty triggers the uncertainty floor (hold at a minimum, do not score further down) rather than abstention or a direct penalty. Thin evidence is NOT treated as a negative signal — Frederick's work is full of projects that started with minimal documentation and grew into something durable. Under-documented projects may be serving obscure communities well; obscurity is not a quality signal in either direction. | completeness < 0.35; no abstention triggered |

C1 (open access) and C3 (anti-extraction) require evidence, not just claims; these cannot be scored on intent alone. C2, C4, and C5 can be assessed partly on design when deployment evidence is absent. Ratio: originality can substitute for at most 30% of the criteria score for assessable criteria. | dossier shows clear design intent but no deployment evidence |
| Movement infrastructure vs direct service | No strong hierarchy. The criterion is not what type of tool it is but whether it genuinely routes value to practitioners and communities. Movement infrastructure that serves well-resourced organisers is not preferred over direct-service tools that serve excluded communities. | applies across all projects |
| Scope of concern | UK/local/community scope is not penalised relative to national or global scope. Projects serving specific marginalised communities in a single city are not inferior to projects aiming for global adoption. Global scope may reduce a score if it comes at the expense of specificity or depth of service to a particular community. | geographic scope information in dossier |
| Dead link cap | Projects with dead homepages (homepage_http_status != 200/301/302 or dead_link=True) receive a ceiling of 45 on their final score. ## Synthesis Notes

### Contradictions audited — none found

1. **C5 (human skill augmentation, +12) vs M4 (reduce: automates civic judgment, −5–8):** These are complementary, not contradictory. C5 rewards tools that augment human skill; M4 penalises tools that replace human judgment. The distinction between augmenting and replacing is clearly drawn in both. A project using ML to surface data for a human journalist (augment) scores differently from one that generates the journalist's copy (replace). No resolution needed.

2. **C1/C2/C3 (reward evidence of openness, practitioner benefit, anti-extraction) vs Popularity discount (high completeness not a positive signal):** The popularity discount flags risk but does not reduce scores. Rich dossiers still provide more evidence for criteria scoring. The discount makes the documentation advantage visible and contestable — it does not override criteria assessment. No operational contradiction.

3. **Underdog protection floor (28pts at completeness < 0.35) vs Abstention threshold (completeness < 0.15 + dead link):** Clean separation. Abstention triggers only below 0.15 with a dead link. The underdog floor operates in the 0.15–0.35 range. No overlap or contradiction.

4. **Prototype handling (protects from maturity penalties) vs C1/C3 (require evidence, not claims):** The procedural rule on novelty vs implementation explicitly resolves this: C1 and C3 cannot be scored on intent alone, but C2, C4, and C5 can be. Prototypes are not penalised for immaturity on assessable criteria but cannot claim open-access or anti-extraction credit without evidence. Consistent.

### Gaps identified

1. **Pure government infrastructure tools** (e.g., OpenCRVS, CKAN): No criterion rewards government-facing infrastructure directly. These projects would need to score via C1 (open access), C2 (if end beneficiaries are citizens not departments), and C3 (if architecture is genuinely open). This is a legitimate constitutional choice — Frederick's work operates outside institutional structures. Government-serving projects that genuinely route value to citizens can still score well; those that primarily serve institutional efficiency will score low. This is intended, not a gap.

2. **Large-scale data infrastructure** (e.g., Wikidata, OpenStreetMap): These score well on C1 and C3 but may not clearly trigger C2 (direct practitioner benefit) or C4 (community journalism). They would land in the mid-range, which seems appropriate for this evaluator's practitioner-first orientation.

3. **AI/ML civic tools**: The constitution handles these through C5 and M4 (augment vs replace distinction) but Frederick has no published views on AI specifically. The constitution defaults to the "serve keen minds, not replace them" principle, which is the strongest available prior. This is the most consequential gap — a more specific AI stance would sharpen scoring in this category.
