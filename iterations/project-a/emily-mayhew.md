## What Emily Mayhew would champion

A project that government departments could actually procure and deploy — that shows up at work, serves people in difficult circumstances, has documented real-world outcomes, and doesn't pretend the institutional context doesn't exist. She would be especially excited by tools that make fair and legible what was previously opaque: procurement transparency, open licensing, accessible public services. She would champion things that close the gap between policy intent and real-world experience for ordinary people interacting with government.

## What Emily Mayhew would discount

Projects that are purely advocacy or research without a pathway to deployment. She would be sceptical of tools that serve already-empowered users, that have no government engagement, or that assume institutions should simply be bypassed. She would also be sceptical of AI projects that claim fairness without any mechanism for affected parties to verify or contest outcomes.

## Proposed winner (primary run)

**Open Digital Planning**

Open Digital Planning stands out as the strongest match for Emily Mayhew's inferred values because it is precisely the kind of project she would want to build: practical government infrastructure designed by practitioners for practitioners, not imposed from outside. It was built by councils for councils — planners at Local Planning Authorities across England working together to transform the digital planning system through open-source tools — which directly addresses the "transferable government skills" question Emily articulates as her reason for joining Newspeak House. The project closes a gap she would recognise from local government digital transformation experience: planning policy has been well-intentioned for decades but the tools available to officers have been inadequate, proprietary, and fragmented. It has real UK government policy outcomes, is fully open source, and operates through a community of practice model that values institutional knowledge alongside technical capability. It would score highly on every criterion: government applicability (deployed by councils), fair access (serves communities through better planning decisions), transparency (open source, open data), practical usability (real deployments across England), and cross-institutional interoperability (designed to integrate across local authorities and national planning infrastructure).
