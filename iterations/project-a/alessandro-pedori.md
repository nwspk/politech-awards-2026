## About Alessandro Pedori

Alessandro Pedori is an AI/NLP engineer, facilitator, and IFS (Internal Family Systems) practitioner based in Berlin. Alongside his engineering career he has built two community tools (Relationship Cookbook, a semi-structured group facilitation experiment; Wetwarecraft, a collection of tools for relational and community cohesion), trained as a Level 2 IFS practitioner and IFIO (Intimacy from the Inside Out) practitioner, and spent approximately 20 years in meditation and embodiment practices including 200-hour yoga teacher training and retreat facilitation. His Newspeak House application positions him at the intersection of these strands: he is interested in voting systems and decision-making formats that "surface hidden consensus and ensure everyone feels heard," and believes "good structures help us collectively become more of who we want to be."

## New dossier fields added

**Field name:** `consensus_mechanism`

**Schema:** String (nullable). Values: `"majority_vote"`, `"quadratic_voting"`, `"liquid_democracy"`, `"pairwise_comparison"`, `"deliberative_polling"`, `"consensus_finding"`, `"facilitated_dialogue"`, `"structured_debate"`, `"prediction_market"`, `"participatory_budgeting"`, `"ranked_choice"`, `"none"`, or a free-text description for novel mechanisms.

**Why this field is needed:** Alessandro's primary criterion — "decision-making formats that surface hidden consensus" — cannot be evaluated from existing fields. `issue_area` tells you whether a project is participatory democracy; it does not tell you *what kind* of mechanism it uses. Projects with identical issue areas (e.g. Polis and Kialo) have radically different mechanisms (opinion clustering vs. structured debate) that would score very differently under his lens. The `consensus_mechanism` field documents this directly.

## What Alessandro Pedori would champion

Alessandro would champion tools that treat collective decision-making as a design problem requiring the same rigour he brings to AI engineering and IFS facilitation. He'd be most excited by projects that reveal what groups actually agree on — not through debate but through structured surfacing — and that ensure every voice in a group has a genuine pathway to contribution, especially quieter or minority voices. Projects with an explicit consensus-discovery mechanism (pairwise voting, opinion clustering, quadratic weighting), theoretically grounded facilitation design, and a non-adversarial frame would score highest. He'd especially prize projects that use AI to make collective human intelligence more legible to itself — helping groups understand their own opinion landscape rather than replacing their judgment.

## What Alessandro Pedori would discount

He would discount tools that digitise existing adversarial processes without redesigning them — petition platforms, standard yes/no polls, partisan debate platforms that amplify disagreement rather than surface agreement. He'd be sceptical of tools oriented primarily toward winning elections or mobilising one side of a divide, as opposed to tools that help a group understand itself. Tools that automate civic outcomes (rather than augmenting civic judgment) would also score low — he values AI that serves human deliberation, not AI that substitutes for it. Projects with no deliberate process design layer — where the "method" is just whoever clicks a button — would rank at the bottom.

## Proposed winner (primary run)

**Polis**

Alessandro Pedori's most precisely stated political technology interest is "voting systems, decision-making formats that surface hidden consensus" — and Polis is the only project in this shortlist specifically engineered to do that at scale. Where other platforms enable participation, Polis reveals the underlying topology of a group's opinions: it identifies not just what each person says, but where the whole group's views cluster, and most critically, which statements command broad agreement across otherwise divided groups. This is "surfacing hidden consensus" as a technical capability, not a facilitation aspiration. The vTaiwan process showed it works at national scale — participants who entered the process disagreeing on ride-sharing regulation left having co-created a framework with legislative outcomes, because Polis made visible the common ground they had not known they shared. For Alessandro, who brings both rigorous AI engineering and 20 years of process design experience to this field, Polis represents precisely what he would call "good structure helping us collectively become more of who we want to be" — the tool does not impose a conclusion, it illuminates the consensus that was already latent in the group. It is open source, AI-powered in a way that augments rather than replaces human judgment, and has demonstrated impact in consequential real-world deliberations.
