## About Asil Sidahmed

Asil Sidahmed is a British-Sudanese policy advocate and public health researcher whose career bridges medical humanitarian operations, human rights ethics, and academic policy analysis. She currently serves as Director of Policy and Health Equity Fellow at the Oxford Initiative for Global Ethics & Human Rights, where her research programme focuses on how technological innovations can be mobilised to reduce inequity in access to healthcare. Before Oxford, she spent years as a Strategic Advisor for Advocacy and ethics committee member at Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) Belgium — working across epidemics (HIV/TB, measles), forced migration, and conflict settings — and co-founded the Sana'a Center for Strategic Studies in Yemen in 2015. Trained in legal-anthropology (BA, Lancaster) with an MPhil in International Development from Oxford (2013), her specialisations include sexual and reproductive rights, decolonising learning spaces, ethics, and accountability for the abuse of power.

## What Asil Sidahmed would champion

Asil would champion tools that make the invisible visible — that document who is being excluded from healthcare systems, who is being bombed in violation of international law, who is controlling procurement budgets that should be delivering medicines. She'd prize projects that are genuinely built for and with Global South communities rather than designed in the West and "deployed" as a technical fix. Technology that strengthens social movements without replacing or depoliticising them — that organises people rather than just informing them — would score highly. She'd be especially drawn to tools that function in crisis and conflict environments: not just scalable in theory, but deployable in Yemen, Sudan, or the DRC with limited connectivity and significant personal risk to users.

## What Asil Sidahmed would discount

She'd discount tools that optimise service delivery within unjust systems rather than challenging those systems. Smart procurement dashboards that make corruption more efficient, or engagement platforms that consult affected communities without structural accountability, would strike her as solutions to the wrong problem. She'd be sceptical of AI tools that collect data from vulnerable populations without explicit harm reduction frameworks. Anything UK/US-only, requiring high institutional infrastructure, or designed primarily for stable-democracy civic engagement would score low.

## Proposed winner (primary run)

**OpenCRVS**

Asil Sidahmed's defining conviction is that health equity is a political problem rooted in structural exclusion, and civil registration is the structural barrier that makes all other exclusions possible — no birth certificate means no legal identity, no legal identity means no access to healthcare, education, or humanitarian assistance, and no access means preventable death at scale. OpenCRVS attacks this problem at the root: it is open-source, deployed exclusively in Global South countries (Bangladesh, Zambia, Senegal, Kenya, Ghana, Mozambique, Sierra Leone, Uganda), and designed specifically for governments with limited technical capacity — meaning it is genuinely built for the contexts she works in, not retrofitted from a Western institutional context. Unlike tools that document the consequences of systemic exclusion after the fact (important as those are), OpenCRVS addresses the pre-condition: giving every person on the planet a legal existence that makes them legible to the systems that are supposed to serve them. For a researcher whose Oxford programme explicitly focuses on "how technological innovations can be mobilised to reduce inequity in access to healthcare," this is the answer in practice — unglamorous, foundational, and structurally transformative in a way that fits her decolonial lens: it is built for LMICs, by a consortium that works with those governments, not a tool designed in the Global North and subsequently deployed.
