## New dossier fields added

**Field name:** `self_hosting_capability`

**Schema:**
```json
{
  "self_hosting_capability": {
    "classification": "full | partial | none | n/a",
    "notes": "Description of self-hosting options and community deployment"
  }
}
```

**Classification guide:**
- `full` — designed and documented for community self-hosting; architecture supports it as a primary use case
- `partial` — open source and technically self-hostable but not primary/well-documented; or cooperative-run service built on self-hostable tech
- `none` — centralised SaaS; no self-hosting option for communities
- `n/a` — protocol, standard, or non-deployable research artifact

**Projects updated (15):** meet.coop, Bonfire, Matrix, Mastodon, Decidim, LiquidFeedback, CONSUL Democracy, Riseup, Radicle, Loomio, Polis, vTaiwan, Citizen OS, Pear by Holepunch, rsky

**Why this field was needed:** Gamithra's highest-weight criterion is whether communities can own and operate their own infrastructure. Existing fields (`open_source`, `governance_model`) capture code availability and governance structure but not whether communities can actually deploy their own instances — which is distinct. A project can be open source without being practically self-hostable (Riseup), or cooperative-run but not designed for self-hosting (meet.coop vis-à-vis individual members). This field makes the self-hosting dimension explicit and scorable.

**Previous fellows:** Connor Dunlop added `ai_governance_dimension` (AI governance focus) to 15 dossiers on his branch — not merged. This field is independent.

## Stage 5 — New field verification

Verified classifications for all 15 updated dossiers. Notable abstentions and judgement calls:

- **Riseup** classified as `none` (not `partial`) because Riseup explicitly does not encourage self-hosting by users — it is a trusted-provider model. Underlying tools (Crabgrass, etc.) are open source but Riseup itself is not a self-hosting toolkit.
- **meet.coop** classified as `partial` because members join the cooperative rather than run their own instance; though BigBlueButton (which meet.coop runs on) is fully self-hostable by anyone.
- **vTaiwan** classified as `partial` because it is a community process using open tools, not a single deployable product. The "platform" is a set of practices around Polis, Consul, etc.

## What Gamithra would champion

Gamithra would champion projects that are simultaneously cooperative in ownership, federated or self-hosted in architecture, and non-extractive in economics. The ideal project for her is one where communities can opt out of the corporate internet entirely — own their infrastructure, govern themselves, and operate on sustainable/regenerative economics. She would be drawn to projects that embody the full stack of the alternative: not just open source code, but cooperative legal structures, community governance, and practical self-sufficiency.

## What Gamithra would discount

She would strongly discount VC-backed projects, however technically impressive. She would be sceptical of projects that are open source but effectively depend on a single commercial operator (no real self-hosting path). She would be underwhelmed by projects that do civic tech through government contracts without questioning the power relationship (i.e. just making government work better without enabling communities to be less dependent on government).

## Proposed winner (primary run)

**meet.coop**

meet.coop is the project that most completely embodies Gamithra's values stack — not just one or two criteria, but all of them simultaneously. It is a worker cooperative (ownership criterion), built on cooperatively owned infrastructure (community ownership), powered by renewable energy (solarpunk alignment), surveillance-free (humane technology criterion), and built explicitly to serve activists, housing cooperatives, and social movements rather than extract value from them. The renewable energy and cooperative ownership are structural, not cosmetic — they are not marketing claims but legal and operational realities baked into what meet.coop is. For Gamithra, the right kind of political technology is infrastructure that communities own rather than rent, and meet.coop is the clearest example in this shortlist of that principle applied to a practical, urgent need: the right to organise without surveillance.
