## About Alexandra Ciocanel

Alexandra Ciocanel is a digital sociologist and Senior User Researcher at the UK Ministry of Justice, where she works on public sector AI adoption, generative AI tools, and AI procurement aligned with user needs and public accountability. She holds a Nuffield Foundation postdoctoral position at the University of York (School for Business and Society) on the Code Encounters project, examining algorithmic risk-profiling in England's private rented sector — particularly how open banking data is used in tenant screening to reproduce housing inequality. She completed a PhD at the University of Manchester in Social Anthropology (2022), with a thesis on liquid homeownership, temporality, and residential assets in Bucharest. She co-founded the Ethnographic Research Hub in Romania.

## What Alexandra Ciocanel would champion

Alexandra would champion projects that make algorithmic systems legible and accountable to the communities they affect — particularly in high-stakes domains like housing, welfare, and justice. She would be excited by tools that combine ethnographic depth with digital reach: evidence-producing infrastructure that can hold automated decision-making systems accountable rather than simply criticising their existence from outside. She would particularly value projects that document how algorithmic systems lock people into future trajectories — the temporal dimension of algorithmic harm that her PhD and EASA 2026 conference work make explicit. She would endorse projects grounded in lived experience and community ownership over abstract efficiency optimisation.

## What Alexandra Ciocanel would discount

She would discount technically sophisticated projects that lack accountability mechanisms or were designed without meaningful engagement with affected communities. She would be sceptical of AI tools that claim to be human-centred but deployed no genuine user research, and of participatory platforms that substitute superficial consultation for real co-design. Projects with opaque methodologies or proprietary decision logic — even with good intent — would score low. She would also be critical of projects that address present harms without accounting for how systems create path dependencies that constrain future options for vulnerable populations.
