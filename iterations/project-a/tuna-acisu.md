## What Tuna Acisu would champion

Projects that make high-quality, open data accessible to people who need it most — especially in humanitarian and global development contexts. Tuna would be drawn to platforms that function as foundational infrastructure (the "plumbing" enabling evidence-based action), that are honest about their data's limitations, and that actively work to correct the gap between what people perceive and what the data actually shows. She would particularly value work that reaches beyond OECD contexts into underserved populations.

## What Tuna Acisu would discount

Pure political campaigning tools, social-movement tech without strong evidence components, and AI projects that make bold claims without methodological rigour. She would be sceptical of tools that are inaccessible to people without technical skills, or that work only in rich-country contexts. She would also discount data projects that exist but don't actually change decisions — the "existence of data is not enough" framing is explicit in her cohort bio.

With over 1,000 organisations relying on it for crisis response data — covering migrations, health emergencies, displacement, and the exact humanitarian contexts that Tuna's OWID work addresses — it represents foundational data plumbing rather than a feature or product. What distinguishes HDX from strong competitors like Gapminder (which scores highly on misconceptions/data literacy) or CKAN (which scores highly on infrastructure) is that it combines both roles simultaneously: it is the infrastructure layer AND the content layer, actively moving data to people who need it in the hardest operating environments. The Gapminder Worldview Upgrader is the closest competitor, resonating with Tuna's "countering misconceptions" angle, but Gapminder's impact is educational rather than operational — HDX's data underpins life-and-death decisions.
