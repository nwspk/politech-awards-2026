## What this person would champion

Aadi would be excited by projects that make the machinery of government — laws, services, procurement, data, identity — actually accessible and legible to people without expert intermediaries. He'd champion tools that demonstrate how updating legal or technical infrastructure produces real improvements in public service delivery: not abstract governance theory, but concrete deployments that have worked. He has particular interest in projects that show how careful regulatory frameworks can unlock the value of emerging technology rather than simply blocking or enabling it.

## What this person would discount

He'd discount projects that are theoretically elegant but have no documented deployment in a real government or public context. He'd also be sceptical of projects that assume only technical experts or engaged citizens will ever use them — his Polici instinct is that if it requires fluency to access, the people who most need it are already excluded.

## Proposed winner

**OpenCRVS**

OpenCRVS is the project that most directly embodies the set of values Aadi Kulkarni has built his career around. Civil registration — the documentation of births, deaths, and vital events — is the most foundational form of public service delivery: without it, people cannot access healthcare, education, financial services, or legal protection. OpenCRVS is open-source digital infrastructure that enables governments in low-resource settings (Bangladesh, Zambia, Senegal, Ghana, Sierra Leone, and others) to provide this infrastructure where it was absent or broken. It is a recognised Digital Public Good, funded by NORAD, the UK FCDO, and the World Bank.

This maps onto Aadi's values in multiple directions. His career began with research on data ethics affecting underserved populations (NSF project) and a startup (Polici.org) designed to break open siloed expert knowledge for communities that lack access to institutional resources. OpenCRVS does this at the level of state infrastructure itself — the absence of civil registration is not a marginal inconvenience but a fundamental exclusion from legal personhood. His Coinbase work shows he believes governments need their legal and technical infrastructure updated to accommodate new types of institutions; OpenCRVS is exactly this for civil registration. It is also internationally deployable and designed for the settings where the gap is largest — consistent with his interest in how tech and law infrastructure need updating for public service delivery not just in wealthy jurisdictions. The open-source, government-partnership model means it avoids the access-extraction dynamic he'd discount, and it has documented deployments (not just theory) across multiple countries at different stages of implementation.
