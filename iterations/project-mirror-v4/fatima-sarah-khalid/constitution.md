## The Two-Axis Framework: Agency × Implementation

V3 asked: *have communities actually deployed and used this system at scale?*
V5 asked: *can communities fork, re-govern, and reshape this system themselves?*

These are distinct axes. High implementation without agency produces institutional dependency. High agency without implementation produces theoretical primitives that serve no one yet. The synthesis framework maps projects across both dimensions simultaneously:

```
                      HIGH AGENCY (v5)
                           ↑
  Promising Primitives     |     Proven Commons
  (forkable, undeployed)   |     (forkable AND deployed)
                           |
LOW IMPL ─────────────────────────────────── HIGH IMPL
(v3)                       |                      (v3)
                           |
  Institutional Artifacts  |     Captured Scale
  (low on both)            |     (deployed, but locked in)
                           ↓
                      LOW AGENCY (v5)
```

**Criterion weights:** Same in both framings — C1 (30), C2 (30), C3 (30), C4 (12), C5 (6), C6 (30), C7 (6).

## Why "Proven Commons" wins under Fatima's values

Fatima's stated shift — "primitives over platforms, agency over participation, adaptability over fixed models" — is sometimes read as a simple preference for agency over implementation. But the synthesis reveals a more precise position.

Fatima does not want primitives for their own sake. She wants systems that *can* be re-governed and *have* proven they work. The v5 constitution is explicit: "a forkable, community-governed primitive that serves ten communities with full autonomy is worth more than a scaled platform that 10,000 communities depend on without control." But this is not anti-deployment — it is pro-autonomy at scale.

The projects that win under both framings are the ones that have solved the harder problem: they are deployed at scale AND community-governed AND forkable. These are not platforms disguised as primitives. They are commons infrastructure — tools the community owns, runs, and can reshape.

This is why the v4 synthesis prioritises **Proven Commons** projects: they satisfy the implementation demand (real deployment, real users, real government adoption where appropriate) AND the agency demand (community governance, forkability, no institutional lock-in). Under Fatima's weights — where C1, C2, C3, and C6 each carry maximum weight — only a project that excels across all four pillars can sit at the top.

## Resolving the v3/v5 tension

The 40 **Captured Scale** projects — well-deployed, implementation-strong, but low on agency — represent the core tension between v3 and v5. Projects like arXiv (v3 rank 6, v5 rank 178) scored extremely well when implementation was the frame: real government and institutional adoption, proven infrastructure. But under v5, the agency deficit is disqualifying: arXiv is not forkable, not community-governed in the federated sense, and its deployment model creates dependency rather than autonomy.

The 41 **Promising Primitives** — high agency, low implementation — represent the other tension: tools like Abstract Wikipedia (v5 rank 3, v3 rank 206) that are forkable, community-governed, and expose their logic, but have not yet achieved real-world adoption at the scale that v3 demands.

The synthesis resolves this tension by averaging scores rather than choosing a frame. This is not a compromise — it is a statement about what Fatima actually values: the combination. Projects that can achieve both are genuinely rare, which is why the top of the synthesis ranking is very close to the top of both individual rankings.

## The synthesis verdict: Bonfire

**Bonfire wins the v4 synthesis with avg score 89.8 (v3 rank: 4, v5 rank: 9).**

Bonfire is the clearest instantiation of Fatima's synthesis values. It is:

1. **Genuinely federated and forkable.** Communities run their own Bonfire instances with their own governance rules. The modular architecture makes it a primitive, not a platform — the components can be assembled differently for different contexts. This is exactly what v5's M_AGENCY boost rewards.

2. **Open source with active community governance.** The Bonfire community governs the project's direction collectively. Under v3's elevated C2 weight (30), this is a maximum-scoring signal; under v5 it is equally central.

3. **Community infrastructure, not surveillance infrastructure.** Bonfire is designed explicitly as an alternative to extractive social platforms — it is decentralised, privacy-respecting, and under community ownership. Under C6 (max 30 in both framings), this is a primary driver.

4. **Accessible to excluded communities.** Bonfire's design philosophy prioritises small communities that cannot afford or cannot trust commercial platforms. Under C1 (max 30), this counts for both framings.

5. **Proven enough to matter, primitive enough to adapt.** Bonfire is deployed across community instances; it is not a theoretical prototype. But its architecture is explicitly designed to be reshapeable — it sits in "Proven Commons" because it has achieved deployment without sacrificing forkability.

The gap between Bonfire and the next-placed projects (CONSUL Democracy at 89.0, Decidim at 87.5) is small — all three are Proven Commons projects. The v4 synthesis does not produce a dramatically clear winner, which is itself informative: at the top of the field, implementation-first and agency-first framings converge on the same set of community-governed, open-source, deployed platforms.
