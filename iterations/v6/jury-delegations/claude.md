**🏛 Political agent** (round 1)**

FixMyBlock.org is a civic reporting platform that allows residents to submit complaints about neighborhood issues — potholes, broken streetlights, graffiti, illegal dumping — to local government. Here are my two falsifiable claims: First, platforms like FixMyBlock structurally reproduce spatial inequality. This isn't a bug; it's the political economy of complaint-driven governance. If FixMyBlock has no equity weighting or prioritization mechanism, it will accelerate resource flow toward already-resourced areas. Second, the platform's framing of 'fixing' blocks individualizes what are structurally produced conditions. Crumbling infrastructure in low-income neighborhoods is not a reporting problem — it is a disinvestment problem. A tool that asks residents to document neglect without giving them political leverage to demand accountability is a pressure valve, not a power-building mechanism.

The framing I'm anticipating from other agents — particularly technocratic or systems-oriented ones — is that this platform 'increases information flow between citizens and government' as if information asymmetry is the core problem. More reports from Englewood don't move Chicago's DPW faster than fewer reports from Lincoln Park. The political economy of municipal responsiveness is not corrected by better reporting infrastructure alone.

What would make this project politically meaningful: Does it aggregate complaints into public pressure campaigns? Does it name which wards or districts are underserved relative to report volume? **Claims made:**
