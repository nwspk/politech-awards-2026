### Modifier 1: Boosts projects that structurally correct a significant power asymmetry
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** strong (+8–14 pts)
- **Applies when:** The project specifically builds or supports formal mechanisms that structurally correct a significant power asymmetry — contestability rights over automated or institutional decisions, enforceable accountability mechanisms, redistribution of decision-making authority to communities previously excluded from it, epistemic redress for groups whose knowledge and testimony has been suppressed or delegitimised. The asymmetry must be significant (not marginal), named, and the correction mechanism must be real (appeal rights with process, audit rights with enforcement, governance with teeth — not disclosure alone). Applies across domains: housing, justice, health, climate, civic participation, information systems.
- **Why Alexandra:** v2 Modifier 1 focused on contestability specifically for algorithmic harm. v3 broadens this to power asymmetries generally, reflecting the C1 revision. Her current HMCTS market rent appeals role is the most direct evidence: she is building digital systems for people to challenge rent decisions in court — a formal legal contestability mechanism, not algorithmic but structural. The SKEPSIS project adds the epistemic dimension: who gets to make legitimate knowledge claims is a power asymmetry too, and projects that provide epistemic redress (e.g., platforms amplifying suppressed scientific or community knowledge against manufactured doubt campaigns) fall within this modifier. Her Code Encounters collective work (HSA blog: "no reciprocal data sharing arrangements to test model accuracy") names the structural accountability gap. [Transform/HMCTS role; SKEPSIS; HSA blog; EASA 2026; Code Encounters; all CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 2: Reduces projects framing expanded profiling as community empowerment
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** strong (−10–15 pts)
- **Applies when:** A project frames increased data collection about communities as "empowerment," "transparency," "data ownership," or "giving communities their data back," while structurally enabling more extensive profiling, scoring, or automated processing of community data — without genuine community control over how data is used, with whom it is shared, or what decisions it informs. The distinguishing question is whether the community has meaningful agency over the data (can decide use, restrict sharing, withdraw consent) or whether expanded data access flows primarily to institutions.
- **Why Alexandra:** The Open Banking paper (Code Encounters collective, 2024) is precisely about this mechanism: empowerment framing masking expanded profiling. The SKEPSIS project deepens this: she has studied how legitimacy tactics work as strategic communication; an "empowerment" framing can function as a legitimacy tactic that deflects scrutiny from the structural reality. Her v3 feedback emphasises critical self-examination of power claims (C4 at 20 pts) as the lens through which framing claims are scrutinised. Modifier 2 is the hard application of that lens to a specific and recurrent failure mode in civic tech. ["Open Banking and data reassurance" 2024 (team); SKEPSIS; all CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 3: Boosts projects serving populations excluded by specific named mechanisms
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** moderate (+5–9 pts)
- **Applies when:** The project's documented beneficiaries are populations excluded from a service, right, or process by a specific, named mechanism — an algorithmic threshold, a documentation requirement, a credit history dependency, a categorical assumption about employment or household structure, a language barrier embedded in a system, a procedural design that assumes a particular life trajectory — not just "underserved" or "marginalised" in general. The exclusion mechanism must be named in the dossier or on the project website.
- **Why Alexandra:** Her research centres categorical thresholds and who falls on the wrong side of them. The distinction between "serves disadvantaged communities" (generic) and "serves people excluded by a specific categorical mechanism" (precise) is the analytical distinction her research is built around. Her ethnographic consultancy work with Roma communities and corruption study subjects adds non-housing evidence: the specificity requirement applies across domains. ["Algorithmic tenancies" 2025 (solo); EASA 2026; ethnographic consultancy; all CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 4: Reduces projects increasing surveillance or profiling capacity without governance
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** moderate (−6–12 pts)
- **Applies when:** A project increases government or institutional capacity to collect, aggregate, or act on data about individuals without a commensurate governance structure that gives affected people contestability rights or formal oversight mechanisms. Applies especially to AI tools deployed in public services without documented accountability frameworks, and to data-collection tools that expand institutional visibility of communities without giving those communities equivalent insight into or control over the data.
- **Why Alexandra:** Her research documents the deployment-ahead-of-governance failure mode across UK housing markets. Her MoJ role (2024–2025) specifically involved developing research frameworks to align AI procurement/deployment with user needs, ethical considerations, and public accountability — inside the government system that could produce the failure mode she critiques. The SKEPSIS project adds awareness that expanding institutional data capacity can also function as expanding capacity for manufactured doubt or epistemic suppression. She is not anti-technology; the specific failure mode she identifies and names is capability without accountability. [Code Encounters collective; "Automation hesitancy" 2024 (team); MoJ/Justice AI role; SKEPSIS; all CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 5: Boosts projects with documented community engagement across the project lifecycle
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** moderate (+4–8 pts)
- **Applies when:** Affected communities — not just professional users or institutional stakeholders — were meaningfully involved across multiple stages of the project lifecycle. Documented co-design sessions, community governance structures, participatory evaluation, user research with target populations, iterative design driven by community feedback, or evidence that community engagement at multiple stages changed what was built (not just how it was communicated). Applies where at least two of the five lifecycle stages (discovery, co-design, consultation during implementation, feedback loops, participatory evaluation) are evidenced. Single-stage engagement receives C3 credit but not this modifier.
- **Why Alexandra:** V2 Modifier 5 focused on "documented genuine community involvement in design." V3 expands this to the full lifecycle, reflecting the C3 criterion revision. Her ethnographic consultancy (60+ locations, 2021–2022, pan-European) and her professional HCD practice (DWP Digital, MoJ, Transform) both demonstrate multi-stage engagement as her professional standard. Her academic epistemology — qualitative understanding of how systems land in people's lives as necessary knowledge — applies iteratively, not just in discovery. [LinkedIn; ethnographic consultancy; Code Encounters; DWP/MoJ roles; all CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 6: Reduces purely technical framings that bypass political/governance dimensions
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** weak (−3–7 pts)
- **Applies when:** A project frames its intervention as purely technical — better algorithm, improved model, more accurate data, faster processing, more efficient system — without engaging with the governance and power questions that determine who benefits and who is excluded by those technical choices. Also applies to projects that treat legal or procedural design as purely technical optimisation without examining how form design and procedural structure distribute access and power.
- **Why Alexandra:** "Valuing the manual" (Code Encounters collective, 2024) is explicitly about naming the governance dimension that purely technical framings obscure. Her current legal design interest — "intersection of legal design and the social sciences to evaluate and design forms" — extends this: forms and procedures are not neutral technical artefacts but designed objects that embed assumptions about who can access a process. The SKEPSIS project further evidences that apparently technical or scientific claims can serve political/institutional functions. [Social & Cultural Geography 2024 (team); legal design interest; SKEPSIS; all CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 7: Boosts projects that examine intra-class dynamics or complicity
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** weak (+3–6 pts)
- **Applies when:** A project goes beyond naming an excluded population in aggregate to examine intra-class dynamics — the relational and structural positions through which some actors within a class benefit from the exclusion of others, or the ways complicity with systems of power operates at the level of service delivery, intermediary practice, or professional conduct. Projects that analyse how actors who are not at the top of a power hierarchy nonetheless reproduce that hierarchy through their own practices (landlords who hesitate but still defer to algorithms; brokers who subvert categories but also enforce them; public servants who follow guidelines that produce exclusion). This modifier rewards analytical and structural precision beyond a simple disadvantaged/advantaged binary.
- **Why Alexandra:** This modifier is new in v3, directly reflecting her feedback that her interest is better framed as class inequalities and complicity with systems of power, not simply exclusion. The EASA 2026 abstract is the clearest evidence: it analyses brokers navigating between enforcing and subverting algorithmic classifications — complicity is the analytical subject, not just exclusion. Her "ordinal tenant" paper examines not just the excluded population but the structural process that produces the ordering. Her PhD on "liquid homeownership" frames homeownership as a class project with temporal dimensions — who has access to homeownership involves intra-class competition, not just rich-vs-poor. [EASA 2026; "Algorithmic tenancies" 2025 (solo); "Liquid homeownership" 2025; all CONFIRMED — new modifier]
