### Evidence Summary

**Career arc and primary anchors.** Alexandra Ciocanel trained as a social anthropologist — she pursued two doctoral projects more or less in parallel: a PhD at the University of Bucharest (completed 2020) on homeopathy and boundary-making in the Romanian medical landscape, and a PhD at the University of Manchester on mortgage borrowers in Bucharest (thesis: "Liquid homeownership: the making of time, class, and residential assets in Bucharest"). She also holds a Bachelor's in Architecture from Spiru Haret University (2004–2011), with professional experience in interior design for offices — this spatial and systems-design background is an underappreciated early formation. This double-PhD trajectory embedded a sustained interest in institutions, categories, and how ordinary people navigate them.

From Apr 2021–Jun 2023, she was a Postdoctoral Researcher at the University of Bucharest as part of the SKEPSIS project — "The manufacture of doubt on vaccination and climate change: a comparative study of legitimacy tactics in two science-skeptical discourses." She led the workstream on climate change scepticism in relation to net-zero policies, conducting comparative UK-Romania analysis using social media analysis. This is significant new evidence: she has researched how institutions construct epistemic authority, deploy legitimacy tactics, and manufacture doubt — directly relevant to her analytical scepticism about organisational framing and empowerment rhetoric.

Also from Jun 2021–Nov 2022, she co-founded an ethnographic consultancy conducting pan-European and nationally sensitive fieldwork: youth perceptions of climate change, perceptions of corruption in local administrations, and access to services among Roma communities. She led participatory fieldwork in 60+ locations and developed ethical research frameworks for working with underserved and vulnerable populations. This is the most direct evidence of her research and ethical practice with communities facing structural marginalisation beyond the housing domain — and of her co-design and facilitation capacities.

From Jan 2022–Jul 2023, she was Research Associate at the University of York on the Nuffield Foundation's Code Encounters project (£212,928, Jan 2022–Oct 2024; she left York in Jul 2023 while the project continued). She organised the January 2023 symposium on "algorithmic dwelling" and co-authored papers published in 2024. From Jul 2023–Sep 2024, she was at DWP Digital, conducting internal user research with cross-functional teams — focused on employee experience (not external citizen-facing services), with full range of research activities: persona creation, user journey mapping, needs mapping. From Oct 2024–Oct 2025, she was Senior User Researcher at Justice AI (Ministry of Justice), working on public-sector AI adoption: designing and leading evaluation research on generative AI for summarisation and redaction; developing research frameworks to align AI procurement and deployment with user needs, ethical considerations, and public accountability.

Her research domains as stated on LinkedIn: housing, household finance, climate change and sustainability, justice, and health. Her self-description: "My practice is eclectic, shaped by sociology, anthropology and human-centred design." Interest in "socio-cultural articulations of time, value, and knowledge." Current exploration: "intersection of legal design and the social sciences to evaluate and design forms."

**Core intellectual position.** Her research constitutes a sustained critical examination of how algorithmic systems in housing markets produce class-differentiated outcomes, complicity with systems of power, and structural inequalities — without accountability. This framing is more precise than "exclusion": she is interested in intra-class positions, the entitlements some groups hold that others are denied, and how complicity with systems of power operates at multiple levels (landlords who hesitate but still defer to algorithms; brokers who subvert rigid categories but also reproduce them). The five major papers from the Code Encounters period (2023–2025) — all co-authored, and therefore to be read as shared intellectual production — document how: automated tenant referencing tools create "ordinal tenants" ranked without viable challenge mechanisms; "automation hesitancy" reveals that human override is present but does not constitute accountability; Open Banking adoption is framed as empowerment but functions as expanded profiling; "valuing the manual" shows that visible human involvement can mask structural automation. The consistent analytical move across all papers is: the appearance of human judgment, transparency, or empowerment does not equal the structural reality of accountability or power. Her SKEPSIS project work adds another layer: she has studied how institutions manufacture epistemic legitimacy to resist accountability — the move from "this looks like accountability" to "this is engineered to appear like accountability" is traceable through her research arc.

**Who she centres.** Her solo paper "Algorithmic tenancies and the ordinal tenant" names the specific populations produced as losers by these systems: "complex tenants" — young people, migrants, people with adverse credit histories, those in precarious employment. But her framing goes beyond simple disadvantage: she is interested in class inequalities, intra-class dynamics, and complicity with systems of power. The EASA 2026 abstract extends this to mortgage borrowers whose circumstances don't fit state category assumptions (non-standard employment, household composition) — and focuses on how brokers navigate between enforcing and subverting those categories, examining complicity at the point of service delivery. Her ethnographic consultancy work adds Roma communities and other structurally marginalised groups to her field of concern, evidencing that her community focus is not limited to the UK housing sector.

**Methodology as value.** Her methodological commitment is qualitative, participatory, and eclectic: 50 in-depth qualitative interviews for her specific strand of Code Encounters; ethnographic fieldwork at a Bucharest mortgage brokerage (EASA 2026); participatory fieldwork in 60+ locations during ethnographic consultancy period; social media analysis for SKEPSIS; teaching at Manchester (social research methods) and Bucharest (ethnographic research, social research methodology, introduction to anthropology). Her stated self-description emphasises the synthesis: sociology, anthropology, HCD, and now legal design. Her career trajectory from anthropology into user research and now legal design is legible as the same epistemological commitment applied in successive professional contexts: qualitative understanding of how systems land in people's lives is necessary knowledge, not optional enrichment.
