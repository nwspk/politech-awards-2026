### Part C: Procedural Rules

**Abstention threshold**
Score N/A only when: (1) the dossier provides insufficient evidence to assess BOTH high-weight criteria (Criterion 2 — centring excluded populations, and Criterion 4 — critical self-examination of power relations), AND (2) the project's accessible web presence also fails to establish any basis for assessing whether it serves excluded populations or examines its own power claims. Dead link with no dossier content = abstain. Thin dossier with any substantive field populated = score with HIGH uncertainty and apply underdog protection if completeness < 0.4. In practice, abstention is rare — even sparse dossiers usually reveal enough about who a project claims to serve to partially assess Criterion 2. Abstain only when there is genuinely no basis for any scoring judgment, not merely because the dossier is thin.

Note: C1 (power asymmetry) and C4 (critical self-examination) are the joint-highest weight criteria alongside C2. If a dossier provides sufficient evidence for one but not the other high-weight criterion, score what is assessable and record HIGH uncertainty for the inaccessible criterion.

**Prototype handling**
Prototypes receive partial protection on Criterion 6 (democratic depth) IF: (a) the project addresses a significant power asymmetry or centres excluded populations, AND (b) the design demonstrates genuine collaborative or empowering intent — not just an aspiration, but a described mechanism for how empowerment or collaboration would operate in practice. Eligible prototypes: Criterion 6 maximum capped at 8 pts (not 12). Theory of democratic engagement can substitute for up to 60% of the Criterion 6 score IF the theory specifically names how empowerment or collaboration would operate — not a generic claim that participation matters, but a described mechanism (e.g., "participants will hold binding decision rights over X via Y process"). Prototypes with no described mechanism for democratic depth receive no C6 protection. This protection reflects her practical understanding from the HMCTS appeals work: building contestability infrastructure at prototype stage is the right time to design in democratic depth, before structural patterns are set.

**Popularity discount**
Documentation richness and civic tech prominence are not quality signals. For well-known projects (Decidim, Polis, mySociety tools, Mastodon, Creative Commons, Wikipedia, etc.): flag popularity_risk HIGH and note in rationale what the score would likely be if dossier richness were normalised (estimate: 8–12 points lower). Do not automatically reduce scores — make risk visible and let readers contest it. Additional caution specific to this constitution: many high-profile "transparency," "empowerment," and "data access" tools in civic tech are subject to Modifier 2 scrutiny and C4 (critical self-examination) scrutiny. Being well-known as an empowerment or transparency tool does not exempt a project from examination of whether it actually shifts power, or whether its framing functions as a legitimacy tactic (SKEPSIS-derived analytical lens).

**Tie-breaking**
When two projects score equally after all criteria and modifiers: (1) Which project more specifically names a concrete excluded population and the mechanism producing exclusion (C2)? (2) Which shows documented community engagement across more stages of the project lifecycle (C3)? (3) Which has a higher democratic depth level — empowers or collaborates vs. merely informs (C6)? (4) Which more directly corrects a named, significant power asymmetry (C1)? (5) Which is more structurally disadvantaged in terms of its own position (C5)? (6) If still tied: higher dossier_completeness ranks higher.

**Uncertainty handling**
Thin evidence does not lower scores. A sparse dossier consistent with the kind of work this constitution values (power asymmetry correction, excluded population centring, critical self-examination) → score held at lower end of the supportable range, not penalised further. If completeness < 0.4 AND the domain is within core concerns (algorithmic accountability, exclusion from public services, structural inequality, democratic access) → underdog protection applies (floor 30 pts). HIGH uncertainty noted in rationale but does not mechanically reduce score. Rationale must state the uncertainty explicitly.

**Traceability requirement**
Every project score must be accompanied by a traceability entry in the consolidated traceability log. For each criterion scored, record:
(a) the dossier field(s) used as evidence;
(b) the specific content of that field (quote or summary);
(c) interpretation — why that content constitutes evidence for or against this criterion;
(d) the score assigned;
(e) the justification for that score (why this level rather than the level above or below).
Partial scores must be explicitly justified (e.g., "scores 8 not 10 because X is present but Y is absent"). Modifier applications must record: (i) which modifier, (ii) the trigger condition as observed in the dossier, (iii) the magnitude applied and why (within the range). This is required for all 321 projects. Scores without traceability entries are invalid.

**Novelty vs democratic depth**
Compelling theory of democratic engagement or genuine originality can substitute for up to 60% of Criterion 6 (democratic depth), but only when the theory specifically names: (a) how empowerment or collaboration would operate in practice — not aspirationally but mechanically (what decisions, by whom, via what process), OR (b) the power asymmetry being corrected and why this level of democratic engagement specifically targets it. Generic "this will empower communities" arguments do not receive the prototype premium. This reflects her HMCTS experience: the market rent appeals design question is specifically about what mechanism allows someone to exercise their right to challenge a rent determination — theory must name the mechanism.

**Movement infrastructure vs direct service**
Neither systematically privileged. C2 (excluded populations) and C4 (critical self-examination) as high-weight criteria tend in practice to favour projects that have direct engagement with affected communities — movement infrastructure that organises researchers, advocates, or institutional actors scores on C1, C3, C6 more than on C2 and C4. This is structural, not a deliberate de-prioritisation of movement infrastructure. Movement infrastructure tools that organise affected communities directly (not just professional allies) can score well on C2 if the community-centring is specific.

**Scope of concern**
Geographic scope is not a primary determinant. Her comparative work spans UK, Romania, and European contexts; her SKEPSIS work was explicitly comparative UK-Romania; her ethnographic consultancy was pan-European. Projects in Global South or non-Western contexts are not disadvantaged. Non-Western context projects should note increased uncertainty when the dossier is thin and the context is significantly different from her evidence base (UK/Romanian housing, justice, climate). Apply values with appropriate uncertainty flagging. Do not assume her analytical framework (class inequalities, complicity, epistemic power asymmetries) does not apply to non-Western contexts — it likely does, but apply with care about specific institutional configurations.

### Part D: Underdog Protection

**Decision: YES (maintained from v2)**

**Rationale (updated for v3):** Alexandra's research is specifically about how algorithmic and administrative systems produce documentation gaps alongside exclusion — excluded populations produce less administrative data, are less visible to profiling systems, and have fewer institutional resources to document their situations. Her ethnographic consultancy work (Roma communities, corruption studies, 60+ locations) is the most direct evidence of her sensitivity to this: the communities she worked with in those contexts were precisely the communities least likely to have their own documentation infrastructure. A civic technology project serving communities excluded by specific named mechanisms is structurally more likely to have a thin dossier for exactly the reasons her research identifies.

C5 (structural underdog signal) now adds an additional dimension to the underdog protection rationale: the criterion is explicitly designed to correct documentation-richness bias in the awards process itself — this is a second layer of protection, separate from the uncertainty floor. C5 actively rewards projects that are structurally disadvantaged. The uncertainty floor protects them from being penalised for thin dossiers. Together, these two mechanisms implement the underdog protection intent.

**Uncertainty floor:** dossier_completeness < 0.4 → minimum score of 30 points.

**Suspended criteria when completeness < 0.4:**
- Criterion 6 (democratic depth): suspended. Do not penalise for lack of documented democratic engagement when the project simply hasn't produced documentation — a project from a grassroots context operating without professional documentation capacity should not be penalised for failing to articulate its IAP2 level.
- Criterion 3 (participatory engagement across lifecycle): partially suspended. Do not penalise for absence of documented community engagement at multiple stages when the project simply hasn't produced documentation — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence for under-resourced projects. Apply a floor of 4 pts on C3 when completeness < 0.4 and the domain is within core concerns.
- Criterion 4 (critical self-examination): partially suspended for under-documented projects — lack of documented reflexivity is not the same as absence of reflexivity. Apply a floor of 4 pts on C4 when completeness < 0.4, acknowledging that the analytical work may have been done without being documented publicly.
- Criteria 1 and 2 remain active even at low completeness: even thin dossiers usually name who a project serves and what power asymmetry it addresses. Abstention applies only if there is genuinely no information on either — which is rare.
- C5 (structural underdog): remains fully active at low completeness — a thin dossier is itself evidence consistent with structural disadvantage, and may provide grounds for C5 scoring.

### Part E: Dossier Field Proposals

| Field name | What it captures | Criterion/modifier it supports | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| power_asymmetry_mechanism | The specific power asymmetry the project addresses — what is the asymmetry, who holds the power, who is subject to it, what is the proposed correction | Criterion 1, Modifier 1 | CRITICAL |
| excluded_population_specificity | Whether the project names a specific exclusion mechanism (algorithmic threshold, documentation requirement, categorical assumption about employment/household structure, procedural design excluding certain life trajectories) vs. generic beneficiary group | Criterion 2, Modifier 3 | CRITICAL |
| lifecycle_participation_stages | Which stages of the project lifecycle have documented community engagement: discovery/research, co-design, consultation during implementation, feedback loops, participatory evaluation | Criterion 3, Modifier 5 | CRITICAL |
| democratic_depth_indicator | IAP2 level of participation: informs / consults / involves / collaborates / empowers — with evidence for the claimed level | Criterion 6 | CRITICAL |
| project_structural_disadvantage | Whether the project itself is structurally disadvantaged — unfunded or minimally funded, grassroots-led, operating without institutional backing, from an excluded community, facing active institutional resistance, or addressing a domain systematically defunded | Criterion 5 | CRITICAL |
| power_relation_theory_of_change | Whether the project articulates how it changes power relations — what specific power position changes, for whom, through what mechanism — vs. only what information it provides or what efficiency it achieves | Criterion 4, Modifier 6 | useful |
| community_design_involvement | Whether affected communities (not just institutional stakeholders) were involved in design — user research with target population, co-design sessions, community governance, design iterations driven by community feedback | Criterion 3, Modifier 5 | useful |
| data_collection_asymmetry | Whether the project collects more data about communities than it gives communities control over — whether the data flows predominantly to institutions or to the communities themselves | Modifier 2 | useful |
| accountability_mechanisms | Formal rights for affected people to contest or appeal automated or institutional decisions — appeal process, audit rights, enforcement pathway, oversight body with real power | Criterion 1, Modifier 1 | useful |
| algorithmic_governance_layer | Whether the project includes explicit governance structures for algorithmic or automated decision-making — not just the algorithm or tool, but accountability structures for how it operates | Criterion 1, Modifier 4 | useful |
| intraclass_dynamics | Whether the project analyses or engages with intra-class dynamics — not just disadvantaged vs. advantaged in aggregate, but the relational positions and complicity structures that produce exclusion | Modifier 7 | nice-to-have |
| affected_community_led | Whether the project is governed or led by the communities it serves | Criterion 2, Modifier 5 | nice-to-have |
| epistemic_power_claim | Whether the project redistributes epistemic power — who gets to make legitimate knowledge claims, whose testimony counts, whose experience is treated as evidence | Criterion 1, Criterion 4, Modifier 1 | nice-to-have |
