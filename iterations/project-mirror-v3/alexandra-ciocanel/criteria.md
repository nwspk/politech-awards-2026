### Criterion 1: Addressing power asymmetries
- **Weight:** MEDIUM (max 12 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** The v2 constitution weighted accountability infrastructure specifically for algorithmic systems at 20 pts. Alexandra replaced this with a more generic framing: how significant is the power imbalance being corrected, and at what phase of the process? This broadens the criterion beyond algorithmic housing to apply across domains and process phases. Her current Transform role is the most direct professional evidence: she is leading UX research for HMCTS market rent appeals — building the digital infrastructure for people to formally challenge housing rent decisions in court. The power asymmetry there is not algorithmic but structural (landlord-tenant, court procedure, institutional form design). The SKEPSIS project adds another dimension: epistemic power asymmetries — who gets to make legitimate knowledge claims about climate change or vaccination — are also power asymmetries. Her academic work (Code Encounters collective, "Automation hesitancy" 2024; EASA 2026) consistently names the accountability gap as a structural problem, but her feedback now positions this as one instance of a broader category. Weight is 12 pts (reduced from 20 in v2) — strong accountability infrastructure alone no longer wins. ["Algorithmic tenancies" 2025; EASA 2026; "Automation hesitancy" 2024 (team); Transform/HMCTS role; SKEPSIS project; all CONFIRMED]
- **High score (10–12):** Projects that build formal mechanisms to redress a significant and clearly named power asymmetry — contestability rights over automated decisions, challenge mechanisms against institutional rulings, epistemic redress for communities whose knowledge has been suppressed, oversight bodies with enforcement power, structural redistribution of decision-making authority. The asymmetry must be named; the redress mechanism must be real (not just informational). Applies across domains: housing, justice, climate, health, civic participation. The test is whether the power position of the affected party has structurally changed, not just whether they are better informed.
- **Medium score (5–9):** Projects that create transparency, disclosure, or access to information about a power asymmetry without building a formal mechanism to challenge it. Genuine contestability infrastructure in early or design stage with a credible operational theory of change. Projects that partially redistribute decision-making authority — involving affected communities in process design without transferring final decision rights.
- **Low score (0–4):** Projects that address power asymmetries rhetorically (language about "empowering communities" or "giving people their data") without structural change. Projects that inform affected people of decisions made about them without providing any mechanism to challenge or correct those decisions. Projects that call human-in-the-loop an accountability mechanism when the human has no obligation to those affected.
- **Dossier fields:** political_relevance_summary, issue_area, governance_model, systemic_issue_area, policy_outcomes, communities_served, accountability_mechanisms, power_asymmetry_mechanism

### Criterion 2: Centring excluded populations by specific named mechanisms
- **Weight:** HIGH (max 20 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** This criterion retains the core framing from v2 but incorporates her direct feedback: (a) the emphasis is on specificity of the exclusion mechanism (not generic equity), (b) her framing is class inequalities, intra-class positions, and complicity with systems of power — not simply disadvantage vs. advantage, and (c) she noted she would also apply it to the awards in themselves. The "ordinal tenant" concept from her solo paper (Housing Studies 2025) is the clearest individual analytical statement: algorithmic ranking produces class-differentiated winners and losers, and the mechanism that produces them is identifiable, nameable, and challengeable. Her ethnographic consultancy work (Roma communities, 60+ locations; corruption studies; youth climate) evidences a research practice that extends this concern well beyond housing. The EASA 2026 abstract focuses on brokers navigating between enforcing and subverting categorical classifications — complicity and intra-class dynamics, not just simple victim/perpetrator framing. This is her highest-weight criterion together with C4. ["Algorithmic tenancies" 2025 (solo); EASA 2026; ethnographic consultancy; Code Encounters (with co-authorship caveat); all CONFIRMED]
- **High score (16–20):** Projects whose primary concern is populations currently excluded from a service, right, or process by a specific, identifiable mechanism — a credit score threshold, a documentation requirement, a data format assumption, an employment-type categorical exclusion, a language embedded in a system, a procedural design that assumes a particular life trajectory. The exclusion mechanism must be named in the dossier or project evidence. Extra weight if the project analyses intra-class dynamics or complicity — not just "excluded communities" in aggregate, but the specific relational and structural positions that produce exclusion, including who benefits from the exclusion of others. Projects led by or in deep partnership with the communities they serve receive additional weight.
- **Medium score (8–15):** Projects with genuine equity framing targeting disadvantaged groups but without specificity about the exclusion mechanism. Projects serving populations with limited existing options even if the targeting logic is generic. Projects that name the population but not the mechanism. Projects that address class inequalities in general terms without tracing the specific structural process.
- **Low score (0–7):** Projects whose beneficiary framing is "citizens," "the public," or generic "users." Projects that serve already-digitally-engaged, resource-rich users. Projects improving institutional or professional experience without named downstream community benefit. Projects that use equity language instrumentally without structural analysis.
- **Dossier fields:** communities_served, primary_users_or_beneficiaries, disparity_tracking, underdog_signal, systemic_issue_area, geography, excluded_population_specificity

### Criterion 3: Participatory engagement across the project lifecycle
- **Weight:** MEDIUM (max 12 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** This criterion expands the v2 "qualitative/ethnographic grounding" framing into a full-lifecycle participation framework — her feedback proposed renaming it "Participatory engagement across the project lifecycle" to capture research before the project, co-design during planning, consultation during implementation, feedback and learning during deployment, and participatory evaluation. Her evidence base for this is broader in v3 than v2: 50 qualitative interviews in her Code Encounters contribution; ethnographic fieldwork at a Bucharest mortgage brokerage (EASA 2026); participatory fieldwork in 60+ locations with Roma communities and other marginalised groups during ethnographic consultancy (2021–2022); SKEPSIS social media analysis for comparative research; HCD methodology across DWP Digital and Justice AI roles (persona creation, user journey mapping, needs mapping); and her teaching of ethnographic research and social research methodology at two universities. Her self-description as "eclectic" (sociology, anthropology, HCD, legal design) reflects a commitment to methodological diversity rather than a single tradition. The lifecycle framing is important: early research before the project ensures the design is grounded in how people actually live; co-design during planning creates ownership; consultation during implementation catches harms before they become entrenched; feedback and learning makes the project adaptive; participatory evaluation creates accountability. A project that does only one of these stages scores lower than one that documents multiple stages. [LinkedIn; Code Encounters; EASA 2026; ethnographic consultancy; all CONFIRMED]
- **High score (10–12):** Projects with documented community engagement across multiple stages of the lifecycle — at minimum, two of the five stages: (a) discovery/research before design, (b) co-design during planning, (c) consultation during implementation, (d) feedback and learning loops, (e) participatory evaluation. Evidence must be documented (not assumed from methodology type). Affected communities — not just professional users or institutional stakeholders — must be meaningfully involved. Projects where community insight demonstrably shaped design decisions score higher than projects where community involvement was consultative only.
- **Medium score (5–9):** Projects with genuine community engagement at one lifecycle stage — user research in discovery, or co-design session, or participatory evaluation — with evidence that it influenced something about the project. Projects with methodological humility that acknowledge what they did not learn from communities. Projects with limited but genuine engagement constrained by resources or access.
- **Low score (0–4):** Projects designed entirely from technical specifications, procurement logic, or assumed need without community engagement. Projects relying exclusively on quantitative metrics without qualitative understanding of context. Projects claiming community focus with no evidence of any community engagement at any stage.
- **Dossier fields:** primary_users_or_beneficiaries, outcome_methodology, documented_limitations, communities_served, community_design_involvement, lifecycle_participation_stages

### Criterion 4: Critical self-examination of power relations
- **Weight:** HIGH (max 20 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** This criterion is raised from 12 to 20 pts (joint highest with C2) in v3 — reflecting Alexandra's explicit prioritisation. The core framing from v2 holds: projects are assessed on whether their claims about what they are doing hold structurally, not rhetorically. V3 adds two new analytical dimensions. First, SKEPSIS: she has studied, systematically, how institutions construct legitimacy tactics and manufacture doubt — how claimed neutrality, claimed evidence-base, and claimed empowerment are produced through strategic communication rather than structural reality. A high-scoring project does not just acknowledge limitations; it examines how its own design might reproduce the power asymmetries it claims to address, and whether its framing is doing epistemic work that benefits institutional actors rather than affected communities. Second, legal design: her current interest in "legal design and the social sciences to evaluate and design forms" — forms and procedures are power-distributing artefacts; a project that designs forms, procedures, or digital journeys without examining how those designs distribute access is doing implicit power work even if it claims neutrality. The consistent analytical move across her career: appearance (empowerment, transparency, human oversight, accountability) does not equal structural reality (power over, expanded profiling, accountability without mechanism, legitimacy tactic). Projects are assessed on whether they have examined this gap in their own design. ["Open Banking and data reassurance" 2024 (team); "Valuing the manual" 2024 (team); EASA 2026; SKEPSIS; legal design interest; all CONFIRMED]
- **High score (16–20):** Projects that actively examine how their own design might reproduce the power asymmetries they claim to address — not just acknowledge limitations, but trace the structural effects of specific design choices. Projects whose theory of change distinguishes between transparency (informing people of decisions) and accountability (giving people power to contest decisions). Projects that name what power relations they are changing and what mechanisms produce that change. Projects that have interrogated how their framing — empowerment, data access, community ownership — may function as a legitimacy tactic rather than a structural shift. Projects aware that their own tools could be captured or repurposed by institutional actors.
- **Medium score (5–9):** Projects with honest documentation of limitations and failure modes. Projects that do not over-claim impact. Projects that show awareness of how their tool could be misused but have not fully traced the structural implications. Projects that acknowledge the gap between design intent and structural effect.
- **Low score (0–4):** Projects that frame data access as empowerment without examining whether affected people can act on the data. Projects whose theory of change rests on "if people know X, they will do Y" without examining whether knowledge translates to power. Projects that call themselves accountable, transparent, or empowering without any examination of what structural conditions would make those claims true. Projects that use participation language without examining whether participation changes power distributions.
- **Dossier fields:** political_relevance_summary, documented_limitations, outcome_methodology, ai_involvement, power_relation_theory_of_change

### Criterion 5: Structural underdog signal
- **Weight:** LOW (max 6 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** This criterion replaces the v2 "Human-centred design practice" criterion. Alexandra's exact words: "I'd like to identify the 'underdogs' among these projects and give some points to those that are structurally excluded or placed in a position of disadvantage." This is a criterion about the PROJECT itself — not its beneficiaries (that is C2), but the project as an actor in the awards process. Is the project itself under-resourced, operating without institutional backing, from an excluded community, addressing a domain systematically defunded, running on volunteer effort, or facing institutional resistance? This criterion directly counteracts documentation-richness bias in the awards: well-funded, institutionally-backed projects with professional communications staff produce richer dossiers and appear more impressive. A project from a small grassroots organisation with a thin dossier and no marketing budget may be doing equally or more important work. Her ethnographic consultancy experience (60+ locations with Roma communities, nationally sensitive corruption studies) is the most direct evidence of her sensitivity to how the most important work is often the least documented. Her own research on how excluded populations produce less administrative data is a meta-level awareness of the same problem. Low weight (6 pts) but important as a deliberate fairness correction for the awards process itself. [LinkedIn; evidence-assessed; CONFIRMED from Alexandra's direct feedback]
- **High score (5–6):** Projects that are structurally disadvantaged in ways legible from the dossier or project evidence — grassroots, unfunded or minimally funded, operating in a domain that receives little institutional or philanthropic attention, run by communities with limited access to documentation infrastructure, facing active institutional resistance or legal risk in their work. Evidence might include: very thin dossier despite clearly valuable work; no institutional backing; work explicitly operating against a powerful incumbent; led by community members rather than professional organisations; addressing a problem domain that mainstream funders systematically defund.
- **Medium score (3–4):** Projects with some structural disadvantage — small budget relative to scope, limited communications capacity, operating in geographic or issue contexts with less access to documentation and reporting norms, early-stage or recently founded. Not visibly under-resourced but not in an advantaged position either.
- **Low score (0–2):** Projects backed by established foundations, international organisations, governments, or major civic tech institutions. Projects with professional marketing and documentation infrastructure. Projects that are already widely cited, well-funded, or institutionally secure. (Note: low score here does not penalise the project — it simply means it does not receive the structural underdog bonus.)
- **Dossier fields:** underdog_signal, dossier_completeness, founding_year, government_partnerships, project_structural_disadvantage

### Criterion 6: Democratic depth
- **Weight:** MEDIUM (max 12 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** This criterion replaces the v2 "Implementation maturity" criterion. Alexandra proposed it based on the IAP2 (International Association for Public Participation) spectrum: informs (lowest), consults, involves, collaborates, empowers (highest). The question is not whether a project is deployed or at scale — it is what TYPE of democratic intervention the project makes. Does it inform people of decisions? Does it consult them about decisions? Does it involve them in design? Does it collaborate with them as equal partners? Does it actively empower them to make decisions themselves? The IAP2 framing connects directly to her current HMCTS market rent appeals work (building systems that let people challenge rent decisions = a form of empowerment), her legal design interest (forms and procedures distribute access to decision-making), and her stated interest in "democratic participation" as a specialism. A project at concept stage that has a genuinely empowering theory of democratic engagement scores higher on this criterion than a deployed project that only informs users of decisions made about them. This criterion rewards the TYPE of democratic aspiration, not just the presence of deployment. [LinkedIn; bio; HMCTS role; IAP2 framework; CONFIRMED from Alexandra's direct feedback]
- **High score (10–12):** Projects whose design and operation is at the "collaborate" or "empower" end of the spectrum: affected communities make substantive decisions, have genuine veto or design power, govern the project, or the project specifically builds infrastructure for communities to exercise decision-making rights they previously lacked. Evidence of collaborative or empowering design must be documented, not just claimed. Theory of democratic engagement that names specifically how empowerment or collaboration operates in practice (not just aspirationally).
- **Medium score (5–9):** Projects at the "involves" or "consults" level: affected communities are meaningfully consulted or involved in design and governance decisions, with evidence that their input influenced outcomes. Participation goes beyond information provision but falls short of genuine collaborative governance. Projects designing tools for collective decision-making, civic participation, or public deliberation — even if not yet deployed at scale.
- **Low score (0–4):** Projects at the "informs" level: the project communicates to communities, provides data or analysis, improves the information available to citizens — but does not involve them in decisions or give them power to challenge or shape those decisions. Valuable but lowest on the democratic depth spectrum.
- **Dossier fields:** political_relevance_summary, governance_model, outcome_methodology, communities_served, democratic_depth_indicator, lifecycle_participation_stages
