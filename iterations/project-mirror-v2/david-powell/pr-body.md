## What is Project Mirror v2?

Project Mirror v2 is a synthetic evaluator estimation workflow built for the Newspeak House
Politech Awards 2026. It estimates how individual cohort members might evaluate the 321-project
longlist based on their public record and provided bio. It is a research prototype — not a
claim about anyone's true beliefs.

It operates as three things simultaneously:
- A constitutional ranking system — each evaluator's implicit values are made explicit as a
  written constitution before any scoring takes place
- A synthetic evaluator benchmark — testing whether AI can reliably infer evaluative constitutions
  from public evidence and apply them consistently across 321 projects
- A simulated jury deliberation system — a fixed 5-model panel votes on the top candidates,
  introducing structured disagreement into the process

**This output has not been reviewed or approved by David Powell. It does not claim to reconstruct
his true beliefs.**

Methodology and design decisions: iterations/project-mirror-v2/process-record.md

## Scoring methodology

Each project is scored against seven criteria, with weights reflecting the constitution's evidence-based priorities. Criteria 1 and 2 (governance structure and user focus) each have a maximum of 20 points and together account for 40% of the total. Criterion 3 (adoption and real-world use) is weighted 18 points. Criteria 4 and 5 (genuine open source, collaborative technology) are 14 and 12 points respectively. Criteria 6 and 7 (public health/government infrastructure, environmental sustainability) are lower weight at 10 and 6 points.

Criteria scores are summed and can reach a maximum of 100 points. Modifiers are applied additively after the criteria total — cooperative structures receive a +6 to +12 boost, VC-backed projects a −6 to −10 reduction, accessibility-first design a +4 to +8 boost, and so on. The final score is clamped to the range 0–100.

**dossier_completeness** (0.0–1.0) tracks how rich the evidence base is for each project. A score of 1.0 means an active website, detailed description, populated fields, and external sources. A score near 0 means almost no usable data. This number is shown alongside the score so readers can audit whether a high score reflects genuine constitutional fit or documentation advantage.

**popularity_risk** (HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW / NONE) flags projects where the score may partly reflect the project being well-known and well-documented rather than genuinely fitting this constitution. HIGH risk does not mean the score is wrong — it means hold it more loosely.

**uncertainty** (LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH) reflects how clearly the constitution's criteria apply to the available dossier evidence. A project with thin dossier data but a specific user population described may score medium uncertainty rather than high.

Note: in this run, all 321 projects scored with dossier_completeness = 0.86 and uncertainty = LOW. This appears to be a data uniformity issue in the scoring pass — the dossier completeness field was not varied per-project as intended. This limitation is flagged in agent-notes.md.

## What David would champion

- **Community-governed open-source platforms** — Tools like LiquidFeedback, Bonfire, and Citizen OS that are built by cooperatives or non-profits and enable genuine group decision-making. These hit C1 (governance), C4 (genuine open source), and C5 (collaborative) simultaneously. David's blog post explicitly names cooperative structure as the gold standard.

- **Public health data infrastructure in underserved contexts** — Projects like OpenCRVS, DHIS2, or vaccine tracking systems that serve provincial health ministries in low-resource settings. His Pakistan vaccination work means he knows what this looks like from the inside — messy, unglamorous, life-saving.

- **Accessibility-first civic tools** — Projects that design specifically for users with disabilities, cognitive impairments, or low digital literacy. BBC Music Memories was built for people with dementia — that isn't accessibility as a checkbox, it's accessibility as the entire product premise.

- **Worker-owned technology cooperatives** — Not just projects that happen to be open-source, but organisations whose legal structure ensures worker control and fair pay. Outlandish and Common Knowledge are David's named exemplars. He'd champion any project built this way.

- **Transparent, non-extractive funding models** — Ghost's non-profit, cloud-funded model with a 50-employee cap. Poteris's flat pay and 4-day weeks. David's framework rewards structural constraints on growth and extraction over impact metrics.

## What David would discount

- **VC-backed civic tech** — Projects funded by venture capital, even if they serve civic purposes. David's blog post argues that VC funding creates structural incentives that corrupt stated missions. The M2 penalty applies regardless of how good the mission statement sounds.

- **"Tech for Good" branded startups** — The blog post's entire argument is that this label is used to attract idealistic talent at below-market pay while maintaining founder control and profit potential. Projects that lean heavily on mission branding without structural accountability would score poorly on C1.

- **Projects with slick documentation but no evidence of user adoption** — David's "John" anecdote describes a government partnership where users never adopted the platform. He'd be suspicious of beautifully documented projects that can't demonstrate real-world use (C3).

- **Performative open-source releases** — Code dumps on GitHub with no community, no contribution guidelines, and no governance. The M4 penalty targets this specific pattern from the blog post.

- **Professional/institutional tools with no citizen-facing dimension** — Tools that serve governments, think tanks, or policy professionals rather than the communities those institutions are supposed to serve.

## Constitutional failure mode

This constitution is built primarily on one blog post and a personal website bio. It captures a strong, coherent evaluative framework — structure over intention — but the framework is inferred from a narrow evidence base. The deepest failure mode is what the constitution cannot see.

David's bio mentions "playful/creative political tech" and "group decision-making," but neither has public evidence to ground them. The constitution therefore has no criterion for creativity, play, experimentation, or aesthetic quality in political technology. A project like a political satire game, a creative civic engagement experiment, or a playful voting interface would score poorly on C1–C7 despite potentially being exactly the kind of thing David would champion. The constitution was built from his most accessible evidence (the blog post's structural critique), which is serious and analytical — but the bio hints at a creative, playful dimension that the evidence base cannot reach.

Similarly, the constitution weights governance and structure heavily because that's what the blog post is about. But David works at Overleaf — a commercial product owned by Digital Science — not at a cooperative. If he applies his own structural framework to his own employer, the score would be mediocre. This suggests either the framework is aspirational (what he wishes the world looked like) rather than absolute (what he'd actually choose), or he has a more nuanced view of when commercial structures are acceptable than the blog post alone suggests. The constitution cannot capture this nuance from one data point.

## Jury run

The jury panel is a fixed 5-model team used across all Project Mirror v2 runs. Each model ran 5 independent evaluations of all 321 projects, for 25 total runs. The panel was selected based on published research into LLM political alignment tendencies: GPT-4.1 (progressive anchor), Claude Opus 4 (centrist proceduralist), Gemini 2.5 Pro (institutionalist/Western-mainstream), Mistral Large (European civic-rights/open-source lens), Grok 4 (disruption-sceptic/right-adjacent outlier). Median aggregation is used to reduce Grok 4's bimodal outlier influence. Grok 4 scores are flagged where they deviate more than 2 standard deviations from the panel median.

**Panel table:**

| # | Model | Role | Political tendency | Key bias to watch |
|---|-------|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 1 | GPT-4.1 (OpenAI) | Progressive anchor | Left-progressive; rewards participatory/justice civic tech | Self-scoring bias — recused from OpenAI-adjacent projects |
| 2 | Claude Opus 4 (Anthropic) | Centrist proceduralist | Rights-based, UN UDHR framing; evaluates process quality | Most likely to give balanced mid-range scores |
| 3 | Gemini 2.5 Pro (Google) | Institutionalist | Western democratic norms; rewards scalability and government adoption | May decline on contested political questions |
| 4 | Mistral Large (Mistral AI) | European civic-rights / open-source | GDPR-aware; favours open-source, privacy-preserving infrastructure | Most sympathetic to European regulatory contexts |
| 5 | Grok 4 (xAI) | Disruption-sceptic / right-adjacent | Challenges progressive groupthink; anti-establishment | Bimodal (67.9% extreme responses); documented ownership manipulation |

**JuryConstGap** is the difference between a project's jury rank and its constitutional rank. A positive gap means the jury ranked it higher than the constitution — potential familiarity inflation flag. A negative gap means the jury ranked it lower. Projects with a gap > 20 ranks are flagged ⚠️.

**Pop Risk** (popularity risk) flags projects where the score may partly reflect the project being extensively documented and well-known, not just constitutional fit. HIGH Pop Risk means: hold the score more loosely.

**Key finding: extremely high abstention rate.** Claude, Gemini, and Mistral abstained on all 321 projects across all 5 runs (100% abstention rate). GPT-4.1 scored 31 projects per run on average (90.3% abstention rate). Grok 4 scored approximately 6 projects per run (98.3% abstention rate). Only 34 of 321 projects received any jury score. This reflects the strict abstention instruction ("abstain when dossier evidence is insufficient to assess the specific constitutional criteria") interacting with the unusually high specificity of David's constitution — which demands governance structure evidence that most dossiers do not contain in structured form.
