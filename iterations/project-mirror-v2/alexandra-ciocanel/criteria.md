### Criterion 1: Accountability infrastructure for algorithmic and automated systems
- **Weight:** HIGH (max 20 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** Her flagship analytical contribution is the concept of accountability gaps in algorithmic housing systems. "Automation hesitancy" (2024) shows that human override is not the same as accountability — hesitancy is contingent and does not give affected people any rights. The HSA blog explicitly names the specific gap: "no reciprocal data sharing arrangements in the PRS to test model accuracy." The EASA 2026 abstract names the same problem in financial infrastructure: "bureaucratic authority over housing access and debt without formal government oversight." Her consistent research question is: who has the right to contest a decision made about them by an algorithmic system? ["Automation hesitancy" IC&S 2024; HSA blog 2024; EASA 2026; all CONFIRMED]
- **High score (16–20):** Projects that build or enforce mechanisms by which people affected by an automated decision can formally contest it — appeal rights with real teeth, community-accessible audit tools, oversight bodies with enforcement power, governance structures that create reciprocal accountability (not just disclosure). The test is whether affected people can act, not just whether they are informed.
- **Medium score (8–15):** Projects that create transparency without contestability — disclosure tools, AI explainability systems, impact assessment frameworks that do not include enforcement. Genuine accountability infrastructure in design-stage with credible theory of change.
- **Low score (0–7):** Projects that improve automation efficiency without any accountability layer. Tools that tell people what decision was made but give no mechanism to challenge it. Projects that frame human-in-the-loop as accountability when the human has no obligation to those affected.
- **Dossier fields:** political_relevance_summary, issue_area, governance_model, systemic_issue_area, policy_outcomes, communities_served

### Criterion 2: Centring populations excluded by algorithmic or bureaucratic categorical systems
- **Weight:** HIGH (max 20 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** The "ordinal tenant" concept (Housing Studies 2025) is her clearest statement of analytical focus: algorithmic ranking produces winners and losers, and her research is about the losers — specifically those produced as losers by the categorical assumptions built into systems. Named populations: young people, migrants, adverse-credit histories, precarious employment, non-standard household structures, people whose lives don't fit the form. The EASA 2026 abstract extends this to mortgage borrowers whose circumstances don't fit state-defined categories. This is not a general equity commitment — it is a specific analytical and ethical focus on exclusion by named mechanism. ["Algorithmic tenancies" Housing Studies 2025; EASA 2026; Code Encounters; all CONFIRMED]
- **High score (16–20):** Projects whose primary concern is populations currently excluded from a service by a specific, identifiable categorical mechanism — a credit score threshold, a documentation requirement, a data format assumption, an employment-type exclusion. Extra weight if the exclusion mechanism is named and the intervention is targeted at it specifically, not at "underserved people" generically.
- **Medium score (8–15):** Projects with genuine equity framing targeting disadvantaged groups but without specificity about the exclusion mechanism. Projects serving populations with limited existing options even if the targeting logic is generic.
- **Low score (0–7):** Projects whose beneficiary framing is "citizens," "the public," or generic "users." Projects that serve already-digitally-engaged, resource-rich users. Projects improving institutional or professional experience without named downstream community benefit.
- **Dossier fields:** communities_served, primary_users_or_beneficiaries, disparity_tracking, underdog_signal, systemic_issue_area, geography

### Criterion 3: Qualitative or ethnographic grounding — does the project know how its users actually live?
- **Weight:** MEDIUM (max 12 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** She conducted 50 in-depth qualitative interviews for her specific strand of Code Encounters when cheaper methods were available. The Romania paper (2025) uses visual-elicitation and life-history interviews. EASA 2026 uses ethnographic fieldwork. This is a consistent epistemological commitment: qualitative understanding of how systems land in people's lives is the necessary knowledge base for designing or evaluating those systems. ["Landlords' and tenants' pathways" 2025; EASA 2026; Code Encounters methodology; all CONFIRMED]
- **High score (10–12):** Projects with documented user research, participatory methods, or qualitative/ethnographic engagement that demonstrably shaped design decisions. Projects that acknowledge the limits of quantitative or technical framings. Evidence that community insight influenced what was built, not just how it was evaluated afterwards.
- **Medium score (5–9):** Projects with user testing or consultation processes that shaped design at a basic level; projects with limited but genuine community engagement; projects that acknowledge methodological limits even if methods were primarily technical.
- **Low score (0–4):** Projects designed entirely from technical specifications, procurement logic, or assumed need. Projects relying exclusively on quantitative metrics without understanding the contexts being measured. Projects claiming community focus with no evidence of community engagement.
- **Dossier fields:** primary_users_or_beneficiaries, outcome_methodology, documented_limitations, communities_served

### Criterion 4: Critical self-examination of power claims — does the project know what it is actually doing?
- **Weight:** MEDIUM (max 12 pts)
- **Why Alexandra:** The Open Banking paper (2024) is specifically about how a technology framed as empowering tenants functioned as expanded profiling of tenants. "Valuing the manual" (2024) shows how visible human involvement can be a legitimating device that masks structural automation. Her analytical move across all papers is: the appearance (empowerment, transparency, human oversight) does not equal the structural reality (power over, expanded profiling, accountability without mechanism). Projects are assessed on whether their claims hold structurally, not rhetorically. ["Open Banking and data reassurance" IC&S 2024; "Valuing the manual" 2024; both CONFIRMED]
- **High score (10–12):** Projects that name what power relations they are changing, not just what information they are providing. Projects whose theory of change distinguishes between transparency (informing people of decisions) and accountability (giving people power to contest decisions). Projects that acknowledge the gap between their framing and their actual structural effects.
- **Medium score (5–9):** Projects with honest documentation of limitations and failure modes. Projects that do not over-claim impact. Projects that show awareness of how their tool could be misused or captured.
- **Low score (0–4):** Projects that frame data access as empowerment without examining whether affected people can act on the data. Projects whose theory of change rests on "if people know X, they will do Y" without examining whether knowledge translates to power. Projects that call themselves transparent without any mechanism by which transparency becomes accountability.
- **Dossier fields:** political_relevance_summary, documented_limitations, outcome_methodology, ai_involvement
