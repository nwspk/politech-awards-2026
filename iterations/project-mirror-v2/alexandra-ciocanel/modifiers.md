### Modifier 1: Boosts projects creating contestability and redress for algorithmic harm
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** strong (+8–14 pts)
- **Applies when:** The project specifically builds or supports formal mechanisms by which people harmed, excluded, or misclassified by an automated system can challenge that decision — appeal rights with real process, audit rights for affected communities, enforcement pathways, oversight bodies with power to intervene and correct. Not disclosure alone: the mechanism must give the affected person actionable recourse.
- **Why Alexandra:** "Automation hesitancy" (2024) specifically names "reciprocal accountability mechanisms" as the structural gap. "No reciprocal data sharing arrangements in the PRS to test model accuracy" (HSA blog) names the specific absence. A project that creates contestability directly fills the gap her research identified as the core problem. [IC&S 2024; HSA blog 2024; CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 2: Reduces projects framing expanded profiling as community empowerment
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** strong (−10–15 pts)
- **Applies when:** A project frames increased data collection about communities as "empowerment," "transparency," "data ownership," or "giving communities their data back," while structurally enabling more extensive profiling, scoring, or automated processing of community data — without genuine community control over how data is used, with whom it is shared, or what decisions it informs.
- **Why Alexandra:** The Open Banking paper (2024) is precisely about this mechanism. Open Banking in tenant referencing was framed as empowering tenants to control and share their financial data. In practice it expanded landlord/referencing company access to detailed transactional data without giving tenants meaningful control over how that data was interpreted or acted on. ["Open Banking and data reassurance" IC&S 2024; CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 3: Boosts projects serving populations excluded by specific named mechanisms
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** moderate (+5–9 pts)
- **Applies when:** The project's documented beneficiaries are populations excluded from a service by a specific, named mechanism — an algorithmic threshold, a documentation requirement, a credit history dependency, a language barrier embedded in a system, a categorical assumption about employment or household structure — not just "underserved" or "marginalised" in general. The exclusion mechanism must be named in the dossier or on the project website.
- **Why Alexandra:** Her research centres categorical thresholds and who falls on the wrong side of them. The distinction between "serves disadvantaged communities" (generic) and "serves people excluded by a specific algorithmic threshold" (precise) is the analytical distinction her research is built around. ["Algorithmic tenancies" Housing Studies 2025; EASA 2026; CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 4: Reduces projects increasing surveillance or profiling capacity without governance
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** moderate (−6–12 pts)
- **Applies when:** A project increases government or institutional capacity to collect, aggregate, or act on data about individuals without a commensurate governance structure that gives affected people contestability rights or formal oversight mechanisms. Applies especially to AI tools deployed in public services without documented accountability frameworks.
- **Why Alexandra:** Her research documents a specific failure mode: deployment of algorithmic systems in public services has outpaced governance development. She works inside government on AI adoption and is not anti-technology — but the specific failure mode she analyses and names is exactly this: capability without governance. ["Automation hesitancy" 2024; EASA 2026; Code Encounters; CONFIRMED; bio; CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 5: Boosts projects with documented genuine community involvement in design
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** moderate (+4–8 pts)
- **Applies when:** Affected communities — not just professional users or institutional stakeholders — were meaningfully involved in the design process. Documented co-design sessions, community governance, participatory evaluation, user research with the populations the project serves, or design iterations driven by community feedback.
- **Why Alexandra:** Her professional identity is as a user researcher and her listed specialisms include co-design. Her academic epistemology is that qualitative understanding of how people actually live is the necessary knowledge base for any intervention. Both the practitioner and academic dimensions of her identity point toward this modifier. ### Modifier 6: Reduces purely technical framings that bypass political/governance dimensions
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** weak (−3–7 pts)
- **Applies when:** A project frames its intervention as technical — better algorithm, improved model, more accurate data, faster processing — without engaging with the governance and power questions that determine who benefits and who is excluded by those technical choices.
- **Why Alexandra:** "Valuing the manual" (2024) is explicitly about this: what looks like a technical question about the human/automated boundary is a political question about accountability and legitimacy. Her consistent analytical move is to name the governance dimension that purely technical framings obscure. [Social & Cultural Geography 2024; CONFIRMED]
