### Evidence Summary

**Who is Alexandra Ciocanel and what is her core intellectual project?**

Alexandra Ciocanel arrived at her current position — Senior User Researcher at the Ministry of Justice (Justice AI team) — through an unusual double-track career: first as an academic ethnographer of housing, finance, and state bureaucracy; then as a public sector user researcher. Her academic work is methodologically distinctive: qualitative, ethnographic, interview-heavy, focused on how abstract systems (algorithms, financial instruments, state classification schemes) land in the lived experience of people navigating housing markets. This is not policy evaluation research; it is social science research that asks what happens *to people* when algorithms become the intermediaries between them and the homes they need.

The Code Encounters project (Nuffield Foundation, 2022–2024) is the clearest signal of her values in action. She spent two years studying algorithmic risk-profiling tools in UK housing — digital tenant referencing products, open banking affordability assessments, pre-tenancy checks in social housing allocation. The research produced four major reports and at least six peer-reviewed papers. The consistent thread across them is accountability: who has oversight of these algorithmic systems, what populations do they systematically exclude, and what role does human judgment play in mediating (or failing to mediate) their harms?

The "automation hesitancy" paper is perhaps her most theoretically important contribution to this period. She found that landlords and agents were not blindly automating — they hesitated. They overrode algorithmic recommendations based on social vibes, personal relationships, and qualitative assessments that the algorithm couldn't process. Her interpretation is not that this hesitancy is a flaw (automation good, humans bad) or a virtue (human judgment always better). It is an empirical observation about how hybrid human-algorithmic systems actually function — and a warning that the "human in the loop" framing can conceal as much as it reveals. Human override is only valuable if the humans are accountable for their overrides.

Her current role at MoJ's Justice AI team extends this logic into the public sector. No direct public writing from the MoJ period has been found (the MoJ blog does not list her as an author). However, the LinkedIn title "Justice AI" and the context of her research background strongly suggest she is working on AI adoption in justice contexts — an area that maps directly onto her academic interests in algorithmic accountability and the conditions under which algorithmic systems produce or fail to produce fair outcomes.

**What does she explicitly value, based on public evidence?**

Her writing consistently foregrounds: (1) qualitative and ethnographic methods as a corrective to purely technical or quantitative framings of algorithmic systems; (2) attention to the populations systematically excluded by algorithmic decision-making (young tenants, migrants, those with adverse credit histories, people in precarious employment); (3) the importance of accountability structures — not just transparency, but mechanisms by which those affected can contest or correct algorithmic outcomes; (4) the value of human judgment, properly constrained, as a supplement to not a replacement for algorithmic processes. She is not an AI sceptic in the sense of rejecting automation; she is an accountability-first thinker who believes systems without oversight structures are dangerous.
