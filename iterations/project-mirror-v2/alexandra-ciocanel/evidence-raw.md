### Evidence Summary

**Career arc and primary anchors.** Alexandra Ciocanel trained as a social anthropologist — her first PhD (University of Bucharest, 2020) was on homeopathy and boundary-making in the Romanian medical landscape; she simultaneously pursued a second PhD at the University of Manchester on mortgage borrowers in Bucharest (thesis: "Liquid homeownership: the making of time, class, and residential assets in Bucharest"). This double-PhD trajectory shows a sustained interest in institutions, categories, and how ordinary people navigate them. From 2022, she moved to the University of York as a Research Associate on the Nuffield Foundation's "Code Encounters" project, which was explicitly about algorithmic risk profiling in housing. She organised the January 2023 symposium on "algorithmic dwelling" and co-authored four reports published in 2024. By 2026, she is working as Senior User Researcher at Justice AI (Ministry of Justice), with prior time at DWP Digital. The bio also identifies her as co-founder of the Ethnographic Research Hub in Romania — this claim could not be independently confirmed through public sources (no website found), but there is no counter-evidence; it is consistent with her Romanian research background.

**Core intellectual position.** Her research constitutes a sustained critical examination of how algorithmic systems in housing markets produce exclusion without accountability. The five major papers from the Code Encounters period (2023–2025) document how: automated tenant referencing tools create "ordinal tenants" who are ranked and sorted without viable routes to challenge the ranking; "automation hesitancy" reveals that human override is present but does not constitute accountability (hesitancy is contingent and could shift); Open Banking adoption is framed as empowerment but functions as expanded profiling; "valuing the manual" shows that visible human involvement can mask structural automation rather than check it. The consistent analytical move across all papers is: the appearance of human judgment or transparency or empowerment does not equal the structural reality of accountability or power. This is her central epistemic contribution.

**Who she centres.** The "algorithmic tenancies" paper names the specific populations produced as losers by these systems: "complex tenants" — young people, migrants, people with adverse credit histories, those in precarious employment. The HSA blog post advocates specifically for people who "fall outside" the categorical thresholds. The EASA 2026 abstract extends this to mortgage borrowers in Bucharest who don't fit state category assumptions (non-standard employment, household composition). This is not a general equity framing — it is a specific analytical focus on people excluded by a named mechanism.

**Methodology as value.** The Code Encounters project conducted 50 in-depth qualitative interviews for the Alexandra Ciocanel papers specifically, plus 120 across the full project. The Romania paper (2025) uses life-history and visual-elicitation methodology. The EASA 2026 paper uses ethnographic fieldwork at a brokerage. This is a consistent epistemological commitment: qualitative understanding of how systems land in people's lives is necessary knowledge, not optional enrichment. Her career trajectory from anthropology into user research is legible as the same commitment applied professionally.

**Professional identity and public sector AI.** The current role (Senior User Researcher, Justice AI, MoJ) and prior role (DWP Digital) situate her inside the UK government digital transformation apparatus — not as a critic from outside but as someone who conducts the user research that is supposed to ensure government AI systems are human-centred.
