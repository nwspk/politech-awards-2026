Alexandra Ciocanel's constitution is built around a single structural diagnosis: algorithmic systems embedded in public services produce exclusion without accountability. Her research — seven peer-reviewed publications 2023–2025, 50 qualitative interviews on UK housing markets, comparative ethnographic work in Romania — consistently identifies the same problem: systems that determine access to housing, credit, and services operate without mechanisms by which affected people can challenge or correct the decisions made about them. Her constitution rewards political technology that addresses this gap directly. It is sceptical of projects that claim to empower communities through data access while structurally enabling more extensive profiling. It centres the people who fall on the wrong side of algorithmic thresholds — "complex tenants," migrants, people with adverse credit, those in precarious employment — rather than generic "users" or "citizens."

## Synthesis Notes

### Contradictions reviewed

**Contradiction 1: Modifier 1 (boosts contestability tools) and Modifier 4 (reduces surveillance without governance) may pull in opposite directions for some government digital infrastructure projects.**

A project building digital infrastructure for a public service could simultaneously create new automated decision-making capacity (triggering Modifier 4 reduction) and include formal accountability mechanisms (triggering Modifier 1 boost). Resolution: both modifiers apply. A project that increases automated decision-making AND builds genuine contestability should receive both the boost and the reduction — the net effect depends on which is stronger. If the governance layer is tokenistic (disclosure without enforcement), Modifier 4 dominates. If the contestability mechanism is real (appeal rights, oversight with teeth), Modifier 1 is the stronger signal. Apply both, note the tension in the rationale. This is not a contradiction to be resolved — it is an intentional feature: projects that do both deserve credit for both, and the tension is informative.

**Contradiction 2: Criterion 6 (implementation maturity, low weight) and the novelty/prototype provision (up to 60% of Criterion 6 from theory of change).**

The 60% novelty tolerance means a project can score up to 3.6 of 6 possible Criterion 6 points from a credible theory of change alone. This is intentional but must be applied carefully: the theory must specifically address accountability mechanism operation, not just assert that accountability matters. Risk of over-application: a generic claim that "this tool will create accountability" should not receive the prototype premium. Resolved by requiring specificity — the accountability mechanism must be operationally described.

**Contradiction 3: Criterion 4 (critical engagement with transparency/empowerment rhetoric) and Criterion 5 (HCD practice) — could conflict for projects claiming strong community-centring without critical examination.**

A project using HCD methods but not interrogating whether its design reproduces power asymmetries should score well on Criterion 5 but poorly on Criterion 4. This is intentional — her academic and professional commitments are distinct, and technically sound HCD work can exist without political reflexivity. The two criteria are allowed to diverge.

**Contradiction 4: Modifier 2 (reduces empowerment-framed profiling expansion) and Criteria 2 and 3 (community centring and grounding) — could simultaneously penalise and reward data-collection tools serving communities.**

A project collecting data about an excluded community with genuine co-design could trigger Modifier 2 if it also expands institutional profiling capacity without community control. Resolution: Modifier 2 is triggered by the absence of genuine community control over data use, not by data collection per se. A project where the community controls the data, decides how it is used, and can withdraw consent is not subject to Modifier 2. The distinguishing question is: does the community have meaningful agency over the data, or does expanded data access flow primarily to institutions?

### Gaps identified

**Gap 1: No criterion for disinformation, media literacy, or information quality tools.** These score on Criterion 4 (critical engagement with transparency) and Criterion 3 (evidence-based approach), but not well on Criteria 1 or 2 unless there is a specific community accountability dimension. The constitution will likely undervalue information integrity work relative to its civic importance.

**Gap 2: No criterion for civic participation or deliberation tools.** Participatory democracy tools (Decidim, Polis, vTaiwan) score on Criterion 5 (HCD in public services) and Criterion 2 (community focus), but there is no dedicated criterion for deliberative design quality. These will score medium but may be systematically undervalued.

**Gap 3: Limited evidence base for AI governance policy tools.** Tools supporting AI regulation, compliance, or sandboxing fit her interests but the evidence about her specific policy preferences is thin. Score on Criterion 1 (accountability infrastructure) if they create enforcement mechanisms.
