Alexandra Ciocanel's constitution is built around a single structural diagnosis: algorithmic systems embedded in public services produce exclusion without accountability. Her six years of empirical research on UK and Romanian housing markets — 50 in-depth interviews, multiple peer-reviewed papers, a Nuffield-funded research project — consistently show the same finding: the systems that determine access to housing, credit, and services operate without mechanisms by which affected people can challenge or correct the decisions made about them. Her constitution rewards political technology that addresses this gap. It is sceptical of projects that claim to empower communities through data access while structurally enabling their more extensive profiling. It centres the people who fall on the wrong side of algorithmic thresholds — "complex tenants," migrants, people with adverse credit, those in precarious employment — rather than generic "users" or "citizens."

## Part C: Procedural Rules

### Abstention threshold
N/A only when: (1) dossier provides insufficient evidence to assess both HIGH-weight criteria (Criteria 1 and 2), AND (2) project's accessible web presence also fails to establish accountability/community-serving evidence. Dead link + no dossier = abstain. Thin dossier with any substantive field = score with HIGH uncertainty and apply underdog protection if completeness < 0.4. In practice, abstention is rare — even thin dossiers usually reveal enough about who a project serves to assess Criterion 2.

### Prototype handling
Prototypes receive partial protection on Criterion 6 IF: (a) the problem domain addresses accountability deficits or community exclusion, AND (b) design shows evidence of genuine community research or co-design engagement. Eligible prototypes: Criterion 6 maximum = 4 pts (not 6). Prototypes with no community engagement evidence receive no protection.

### Popularity discount
Popularity and documentation richness are not quality signals. For well-known civic tech projects (Decidim, Polis, mySociety tools, Creative Commons, etc.): flag popularity_risk HIGH, note in rationale that score would be 8–12 points lower if dossier richness were normalised. Do not automatically reduce — make risk visible. Additional caution: many praised "transparency" or "empowerment" tools in civic tech are subject to Modifier 2 scepticism — assess on accountability and community-centring credentials, not reputation.

### Tie-breaking
Equal scores: (1) Which project more specifically names a concrete excluded population rather than a generic one? (2) Which has documented community involvement in design? (3) Which creates contestability mechanisms vs. only transparency? (4) If still tied: higher dossier_completeness ranks higher.

### Uncertainty handling
Thin evidence does not reduce scores. Sparse dossier consistent with accountability/community-centring focus → score held at lower end of supportable range, not penalised further. If completeness < 0.4 AND domain is within core concerns → underdog protection applies (floor 30 pts). HIGH uncertainty noted in rationale but does not mechanically reduce score.

### Novelty vs implementation
Theory of change can substitute for up to 60% of Criterion 6 (implementation) — higher novelty tolerance than implementation-oriented evaluators, because accountability infrastructure for algorithmic systems barely exists anywhere and requiring extensive deployment evidence would structurally disadvantage the most important work. Theory of change must be specific about how the accountability mechanism changes power relationships.

### Movement infrastructure vs direct service
Neither systematically privileged. But Criteria 1 and 2 (HIGH weight) tend to favour direct service tools in practice, because they ask whether the tool creates accountability pathways, not whether it contributes to scholarship about their absence. Movement infrastructure tools serving researchers/advocates score on Criteria 3–5 more than Criteria 1–2.

### Scope of concern

Projects in Global South contexts are not disadvantaged. ## Synthesis Notes

### Contradictions reviewed

**Potential contradiction 1: Modifier 1 (boosts contestability tools) and Modifier 4 (reduces surveillance capacity increase) may pull in opposite directions for some government digital infrastructure projects.**
A project that builds digital infrastructure for a public service could simultaneously create new algorithmic decision-making capacity (triggering Modifier 4 reduction) and include formal accountability mechanisms (triggering Modifier 1 boost). Resolution: the modifiers work as designed — a project that increases automated decision-making AND builds in contestability mechanisms should receive both. The net effect depends on the relative presence of each element. If both are strongly present, the modifiers partially cancel. If the governance layer is weak or tokenistic, Modifier 4 dominates. Apply both, note the tension in the rationale.

**Potential contradiction 2: Criterion 6 (implementation maturity — low weight) and Procedural rule on novelty vs implementation (up to 60% of Criterion 6 from theory of change alone).**
The 60% novelty tolerance means a project can score up to 3.6 of 6 possible Criterion 6 points purely from a credible theory of change. This is intentional but should be applied carefully: the theory must specifically address how an accountability mechanism would operate, not just assert that accountability matters.

**Potential contradiction 3: Criterion 4 (critical engagement with transparency rhetoric) and Criterion 5 (HCD practice) — could conflict for projects claiming strong community-centring without critical examination.**
A project that uses HCD methods but doesn't interrogate whether its design reproduces power asymmetries should score well on Criterion 5 but poorly on Criterion 4. This is intentional — her academic and professional commitments are distinct, and some HCD-labelled work is technically sound without being politically reflexive.

**Potential contradiction 4: Underdog protection (YES) with community-centring criterion (Criterion 2 HIGH weight).**
Underdog protection prevents penalising projects for thin dossiers. But Criterion 2 requires evidence that excluded communities are specifically served. Resolution: underdog protection suspends Criterion 6 (implementation) and partially suspends Criterion 3 (methodology evidence), but does NOT suspend Criterion 2. Even thin dossiers should name who they serve. If a thin dossier provides absolutely no information about who benefits, the project is scored with HIGH uncertainty and the lower end of the uncertainty floor, but Criterion 2 remains applicable.

### Gaps identified

**Gap 1: No criterion for disinformation, media literacy, or information quality tools.**
Tools like Full Fact, Bellingcat, or media literacy platforms don't directly address algorithmic accountability or community exclusion. They score primarily on Criterion 4 (critical engagement with transparency) and Criterion 3 (evidence-based approach), but won't score well on Criteria 1 or 2 unless there's a specific community accountability dimension. The constitution will likely undervalue information integrity work relative to its civic importance.

**Gap 2: No criterion for civic participation or deliberation tools.**
Participatory democracy tools (Decidim, Polis, vTaiwan) are not specifically about algorithmic accountability or community exclusion from services. They score on Criterion 5 (HCD in public services) and Criterion 2 (community involvement), but there's no dedicated criterion for deliberative design quality. These will score medium under this constitution but may be systematically undervalued.

**Gap 3: Limited evidence base for AI governance policy tools.**
Tools that support AI governance, regulatory analysis, or policy development (e.g., AI Act compliance tools, regulatory sandboxes) fit her interests but the evidence is thin about her specific policy preferences. She is not an AI governance policy specialist in her published record — she's an empirical researcher who documents harms. Policy tools score on Criterion 1 (accountability infrastructure) if they create enforcement mechanisms, but the evidence base for scoring nuanced policy instrument design is weaker than for accountability/community-centring criteria.
