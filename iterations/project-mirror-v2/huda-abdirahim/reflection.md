## 1. WHAT HUDA WOULD CHAMPION

The projects that score highest under this constitution share a specific structural profile: they make collective resources or collective decision-making legible to those who own or are affected by them. Aragon leads the constitutional ranking because it does this through on-chain, cryptographically verifiable mechanisms — the rules are encoded, auditable, and not contingent on trusting an administrator. The DAO Framework, LiquidFeedback, and Bonfire follow because they share the same commitment to governance as visible architecture rather than invisible process. What pushes a project into the top tier here is the simultaneous presence of three things: treasury or budget transparency as a design principle (not a compliance feature), governance legibility to those affected rather than just to administrators, and some form of collective ownership over the infrastructure itself. Projects that do any one of these well land in the mid-range. Projects that do all three — and do so through open, interoperable standards rather than locked-in proprietary platforms — approach the top. Cobudget and Open Collective score well because collective resource allocation is their core function, not a side feature. The strongest signal in this constitution is the question: who does the transparency serve, and who controls the infrastructure? The near-perfect project is a deployed cooperatively-governed platform using cryptographically verifiable transparency that lets communities audit both their own treasuries and the governance decisions that affect them, with a published power architecture.

## 2. WHAT HUDA WOULD DISCOUNT

The constitution is structurally incapable of seeing much of what mainstream civic tech does well. Projects in the bottom quarter share one feature: they make political processes more legible to observers or analysts without redistributing governance power to those affected. PolicyMogul (parliamentary monitoring for lobbyists), WorkInCharities (employment tools for the voluntary sector), and The Decelerator (tech-slowing advocacy) all sit at the bottom not because they are valueless — they are clearly useful in their domains — but because the constitution is looking for something specific: collective ownership, treasury legibility, programmable governance. Tools that improve individual navigation of political systems without touching the underlying power distribution score poorly. Consumer-facing civic apps that aggregate citizen data for government use, or platforms that serve professional democratic stakeholders without distributing governance rights, will be systematically discounted. Election monitoring tools, media literacy platforms, and government service delivery apps occupy a middle range — they score on governance legibility (Criterion 2) but not on collective ownership (Criterion 3) or treasury transparency (Criterion 1), which carry the heaviest weights. The discount is not a judgment that these tools are bad; it reflects where the constitution's highest-weight criteria point.

## 3. CONSTITUTIONAL FAILURE MODE

The constitution cannot see the value of democratic legitimacy built through mass participation without collective ownership structures. Tools like Decidim, vTaiwan, and CONSUL Democracy rank high in the jury precisely because they enable large-scale deliberation — but in the constitutional ranking they score in the mid-50s or lower because they do not score well on treasury transparency (Criterion 1) or collective ownership (Criterion 3). The mechanism of the failure is this: the constitution defines legitimacy through ownership and financial legibility. But a significant strand of democratic theory holds that legitimacy comes from inclusive participation — that a million people having a voice in a deliberative platform creates democratic legitimacy even if that platform is foundation-funded and centrally governed. Huda's constitution, built from her work in DAO treasury analytics and collective finance, does not have room for this. It is not that she would reject participatory democracy — it is that the criteria are designed around financial and governance legibility in the DAO/collective-ownership tradition, which is a specific school within political infrastructure. Mainstream deliberative democracy tools, even those that have achieved extraordinary scale and legitimacy, land in the mid-range because they are "governance" without "collective ownership" as this constitution defines it. The large jury-constitution gaps on vTaiwan, Decidim, and CONSUL are the clearest empirical signal of this blind spot.
