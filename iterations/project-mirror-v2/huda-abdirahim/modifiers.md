### Modifier 1: Boost — programmable governance and on-chain transparency

- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** strong (+8–12 points)
- **Applies when:** The project uses programmable, on-chain, or cryptographically verifiable mechanisms to enforce transparency, governance accountability, or collective resource management. The mechanism must be functional (not aspirational) and oriented toward public legibility rather than efficiency.
- **Why Huda:** "Programmable governance" is explicitly listed as an interest area. TreasureCorp's product is built specifically on the premise that on-chain data creates transparency that traditional financial tools cannot. This is not a generic enthusiasm for blockchain — it is a specific interest in programmable mechanisms that enforce accountability as a property of the system rather than relying on human goodwill. ### Modifier 2: Boost — tools that serve those excluded from traditional financial/governance systems

- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** moderate (+5–8 points)
- **Applies when:** The project explicitly serves communities who have been shut out of traditional financial infrastructure, governance participation, or civic institutions — due to geography, documentation status, economic exclusion, or structural marginalisation. The exclusion must be named and the tool must directly address it.
- **Why Huda:** "Collective finance" and "collective ownership" as values, combined with Kenya-based context and DAO governance focus, suggest awareness that traditional financial and governance infrastructure excludes many potential participants. TreasureCorp addresses the gap between decentralised resource ownership and legible governance — a gap that matters most to communities for whom traditional banking and governance institutions are inaccessible or inadequate. ### Modifier 3: Boost — projects that make the relationship between code and power explicit

- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** moderate (+4–7 points)
- **Applies when:** The project includes explicit design attention to how its technical architecture distributes or concentrates power — and makes this visible to users, communities, or the public. Not just "we are open-source" but "here is how this system creates or limits accountability, and here is how you can see it."
- **Why Huda:** "Code shapes power and that relationship needs to be made visible and accountable" is the most distinctive value claim in the bio. It goes beyond transparency-as-compliance into a structurally reflexive concern: technology systems are power systems, and the relationship should be legible. Projects that embody this reflexivity — that name their own power architecture and make it inspectable — are distinct from projects that simply happen to be open. [bio, CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 4: Reduce — extractive platforms and surveillance-adjacent tools

- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** strong (−8–12 points)
- **Applies when:** The project's core model involves extracting data from communities, monetising collective activity, or increasing surveillance capacity without accountability to those surveilled. Tools that aggregate civic data for third-party commercial purposes. Platforms that present as community tools but derive value from community activity for non-community benefit.
- **Why Huda:** The explicit commitment to collective ownership and democratic governance of resources directly conflicts with extractive models. A person building transparency infrastructure for collective finance would apply a sharp critical lens to tools that centralise ownership while claiming community benefit. [bio, CONFIRMED — by inference from stated values]

### Modifier 5: Reduce — digital governance tools that increase state power without accountability

- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** moderate (−5–8 points)
- **Applies when:** The project enables government or institutional actors to manage, monitor, or control civic activity without a corresponding increase in accountability to those affected. The reduction applies when power concentration is the mechanism — not when the tool serves government without creating power asymmetry.
- **Why Huda:** "Code shapes power and that relationship needs to be made visible and accountable" applies in both directions — not just to collective/community governance but to state/institutional power. Tools that increase state capacity without accountability violate the principle as directly as tools that obscure collective finance. [bio, CONFIRMED — by inference from accountability framing]

### Modifier 6: Conditional — early-stage or prototype tools with strong theory of change

- **Direction:** conditional boost
- **Magnitude:** weak (+3–5 points)
- **Applies when:** The project is at prototype or early-deployment stage but has a clearly articulated theory of change grounded in the specific technical or governance gap it addresses. The theory of change must be specific (not "we will increase civic participation") and the technical approach must be credible given the team's apparent capacity. Does NOT apply when the prototype is purely theoretical.
- **Why Huda:** TreasureCorp is itself an early-stage product (beta programme, limited deployment). A builder of early-stage tools is likely to extend some patience to other early-stage tools with credible directions of travel. However, the modifier is conditional because the TreasureCorp product has a specific, technically grounded approach — the tolerance is for credible prototypes, not vague concepts.
