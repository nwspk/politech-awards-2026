## What Jamie Would Champion

Jamie's constitution rewards a very specific archetype: open civic infrastructure that operates at the intersection of government deployment and participatory governance, with explicit safety or interpretability mechanisms. The top-ranked projects — Interoperable Deliberative Tools, Open Digital Planning, AlgorithmWatch — all combine public-interest deployment (C2) with either open infrastructure (C3) or systemic risk awareness (C6), and then receive modifier boosts for being community-enabling infrastructure (M3) or having participatory governance elements (M4). The constitution's sweet spot is a project like OpenCRVS or CKAN: open-source, government-deployed, serving populations who need the tool to exercise basic rights, with documentation that makes it replicable. Projects that additionally address AI safety or algorithmic accountability (C1) get a significant M1 boost when deployed in high-stakes government contexts — this is the distinctive Jamie signature. AlgorithmWatch scores highly because it combines systemic risk monitoring (C6, 12pts) with accountability mechanisms, hitting both the safety criterion and the safety-prerequisite modifier. The constitution is built for someone who thinks tools should be open, interpretable, and serving the people they affect — and whose career has been about making that happen concretely.

---

## What Jamie Would Discount

The constitution systematically undervalues three categories. First, privacy and infrastructure tools that serve individuals rather than communities — projects like Plausible Analytics, Tor, and Signal score low because C2 (public-interest deployment) rewards named beneficiary populations and government contexts, not individual users making private choices. These are genuinely important political technology, but the constitution's lens is "AI for public good" not "tools for individual autonomy." Second, cultural and symbolic projects — the Society for Hopeful Technologists, artistic interventions, movement-building through narrative rather than infrastructure — score near zero because no criterion captures cultural change as a mechanism. Third, and most interestingly, projects that are deeply proprietary but genuinely impactful: Yoti (digital identity verification), DeepSeek-V3 (large language model research), and commercial civic tech platforms score low on C3 (open infrastructure) and trigger M6 (ethics rhetoric penalty) when they claim responsibility without showing mechanisms. The discount on proprietary tools is deliberate — Jamie's career is built on open, community-usable tooling — but it means the constitution cannot see the value of a well-deployed proprietary tool that actually serves vulnerable populations effectively.

---

## Constitutional Failure Mode

The constitution's primary blind spot is **mature, non-open-source direct-service tools that serve vulnerable populations in non-English-speaking, non-government contexts.** A tool like a community health worker app deployed across rural India, built by a social enterprise, not open-source, not government-contracted, but serving millions — would score poorly on C3 (no open infrastructure), get no M3 boost (not community infrastructure), score moderately on C2 (beneficiaries exist but not government-facing), and receive no M1 boost (no explicit safety mechanism despite high-stakes deployment). The constitution is calibrated to the world Jamie works in: UK government, open-source Python libraries, AI Safety Camp. It rewards legibility to the open-source and government-contracting ecosystem. Projects that are legible to the communities they serve but opaque to this ecosystem fall through the cracks — not because Jamie wouldn't value them, but because the criteria have no pathway to see them.
