### Evidence summary

**What the record shows clearly:**

Jamie Coombes entered the data world from an unusual route: Physics and Atmospheric Physics at Imperial (with a cyclone formation research component), then a year teaching secondary school science via Teach First before joining Coefficient.ai initially as an intern in 2021. This teaching background recurs in his GitHub bio: "Taught-First." — suggesting he consciously codes for legibility and pedagogy, not just performance. He frames his work as "Making Transformers Obvious" — a didactic instinct applied to interpretability.

His core technical identity is built around transformer interpretability. The `obvs` library (implementing Patchscope lenses for mechanistic interpretability) was his primary AI Safety Camp project and appears to be his most sustained original contribution. The AISC9 team tackled a real problem in the interpretability tooling ecosystem: Patchscopes was a strong research framework from Google PAIR but lacked clean Python packaging. The result — an MIT-licensed PyPI library with documentation hosted on ReadTheDocs — is the kind of infrastructure-building work that sits at the intersection of technical research and community enablement. This is not just doing interpretability research; it's making interpretability research more accessible.

His government AI work at Coefficient is confirmed but opaque at the individual project level. The GOV.UK case study confirms Coefficient did law enforcement ML (facial recognition redaction evaluation, crime pattern modelling) and AI ethics work with DSIT. The bio lists FCDO, Home Office, DCMS, and DBT — these department attributions come from the provided bio and cannot be independently corroborated per source (government procurement is rarely attributed to named junior staff). The institutional context (Coefficient on ACE framework, John Sandall's Newspeak House connections) makes the government work credible even where specific projects can't be verified at the individual level.

His four conference talks span 2023–2025: PyCon Limerick (LLMs), EuroPython Prague ("Language Model Zen"), PyConDE 2024 ("Mojo as LLM dev tool"), and PyConDE 2025 (Rust/Python async). The talks position him as a practitioner-communicator rather than a pure researcher — someone who bridges tool development and community explanation. The AI Safety Camp framing of the Mojo talk is notable: he explicitly describes building an LLM interpretation library as the AI Safety Camp project, contextualising a language performance talk within a safety research frame.

The AI-Risk-Survey repository is the clearest public evidence of his risk thinking: a synthetic user research study using local LLMs to model AI risk perceptions. This is methodologically interesting — using AI to simulate diverse stakeholder views on AI risk — and suggests he thinks empirically about risk rather than purely philosophically.

The 2026 tech-for-good hackathon project (999 call transcription/clustering) shows active interest in AI for emergency services — a very concrete public-interest application. This is consistent with the stated "AI for public good" interest from the bio.

The `obvs` project is framed instrumentally (making research tools more accessible) rather than making specific safety claims.
