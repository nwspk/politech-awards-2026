## 3. Constitutional Failure Mode

The built-in blind spot is **technology evaluation without technology expertise**. This constitution evaluates through governance, equity, decolonial, and ethical lenses — but it cannot assess whether a tool is technically well-built, architecturally sound, appropriately designed for its context, or innovatively solving a hard engineering problem. The constitution's own synthesis notes acknowledge this: "This constitution evaluates through governance, equity, and ethical lenses. It is weak at evaluating technical quality, scalability, code quality, or technical innovation."

The failure mechanism is structural. Asil's public evidence base contains no GitHub activity, no software projects, no technical writing, no engagement with open-source licensing or software architecture. Her Oxford Initiative research asks "how technological innovations can be mobilised to reduce inequity in access to healthcare" — but the emphasis is on "inequity in access" not on the technological innovations themselves. This means the constitution can identify which problems matter but cannot assess whether a given tool is a good solution to those problems. Two projects addressing maternal health in conflict zones — one with a robust, privacy-preserving architecture and one with a fragile, data-leaking prototype — would score similarly under this constitution if their dossier descriptions are similar. The ethical infrastructure criterion (Criterion 4, 15pts) partially captures this, but only when the dossier explicitly documents consent frameworks and data protection — which most dossiers do not.

The practical consequence is that the ranking may systematically over-value well-described projects with the right keywords and under-value technically excellent projects that describe themselves in engineering rather than governance language. This is the inverse of the typical political technology bias (which over-values technical sophistication and under-values governance design). It is a coherent reflection of Asil's values — but it means the ranking should be read as "which projects align with health equity and decolonial governance priorities" rather than "which projects are the best political technology."
