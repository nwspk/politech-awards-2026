## What Chris Owen Would Champion

The projects that score highest under this constitution share a specific profile: they are open-source or openly licensed, operated partly or wholly through volunteer or low-cost models, and they either directly serve communities excluded from mainstream technology access or actively build the capacity of people in those communities to participate in civic life. Humble Data Workshop tops the ranking precisely because it sits at the intersection of data literacy education, open materials, and a focus on underrepresented groups in data science — it is, in miniature, what SHA and CYF tried to be in their respective domains. Teaching Public Service in the Digital Age scores highly for similar reasons: it is a capacity-building resource for public servants, treating them as learners rather than passive implementers. Mozilla Data Collective's top-three finish reflects the open educational infrastructure and explicitly excluded-populations framing, though the constitution docks it for treating communities as data sources rather than building their independent capability. What these projects have in common is an implicit theory of change that Owen has demonstrated through his career: the way technology creates positive impact is not by being used, but by being understood and owned by the people it is supposed to serve. Projects with strong deployment footprints but no empowerment logic — government platforms, policy tools, transparency tools serving journalists — cluster in the 40-60 range. They are recognised but not championed.

## What Chris Owen Would Discount

The projects ranked lowest are those that serve already-enfranchised users more efficiently or that digitise existing institutional processes without expanding who can participate in them. PolicyMogul, Overton, GovWise, and PostBug — all in the bottom 15 — are proprietary tools serving professional political audiences: lobbyists, policy analysts, civil servants. They may be technically excellent and genuinely useful to their users, but the constitution's Modifier 3 (reduces projects that digitise existing power structures without expanding access) actively depresses them, and C1 (accessibility for excluded communities) and C2 (empowerment logic) cannot compensate because neither criterion is met. Public Editor and OpenAudience land at the very bottom not because they are low-quality projects but because the constitution literally cannot see why they matter: the criteria are calibrated around a different set of problems. There is also a subtler discount operating on participation platforms: Decidim (rank 48), Loomio (rank 158), CONSUL Democracy (rank 53), and Citizen OS (rank 47) are all open-source, community-governed, deployed participatory democracy tools. They sit in the 42-56 range, penalised by C2's education-specific framing of empowerment. The constitution cannot easily distinguish between a platform that gives people a button to push and a platform that genuinely changes how people understand and shape collective decisions. This is the constitution's most significant discount.

---

## Constitutional Failure Mode

The constitution cannot see political technology that operates through participation rather than through education. C2 (20 points, highest weight tied with C1) defines empowerment as building lasting capability through learning — leaving people more able to act than before they engaged. This is drawn almost entirely from Owen's career as a coding educator and the single confirmed first-person quote about the moment a learner "realises they can do it." But participatory democracy platforms operate through a different theory of empowerment: they change the structures through which decisions are made, giving people a formal role in governance they did not previously have. Decidim, CONSUL Democracy, Loomio, Citizen OS, Rahvaalgatus — these platforms are doing something Owen would very plausibly care about as a Newspeak House fellow studying political technology. But the constitution's C2 criterion scores passive participation (click a button, cast a vote) as low-empowerment, because it cannot distinguish between a well-designed deliberative platform and a checkbox survey. The failure mode is not that Owen would dismiss these projects — it is that the constitution was inferred from his career before his fellowship, and his fellowship may have substantially expanded or shifted his evaluative lens for exactly this category of work. Every participation platform on the longlist is caught in a blind spot that the evidence base cannot resolve.
