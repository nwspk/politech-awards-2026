## 1. What is Project Mirror v2?

Project Mirror v2 is a synthetic evaluator estimation workflow built for the Newspeak House
Politech Awards 2026. It estimates how individual cohort members might evaluate the 321-project
longlist based on their public record and provided bio. It is a research prototype — not a
claim about anyone's true beliefs.

It operates as three things simultaneously:
- A constitutional ranking system — each evaluator's implicit values are made explicit as a
  written constitution before any scoring takes place
- A synthetic evaluator benchmark — testing whether AI can reliably infer evaluative constitutions
  from public evidence and apply them consistently across 321 projects
- A simulated jury deliberation system — a fixed 5-model panel votes on the top candidates,
  introducing structured disagreement into the process

her true beliefs.**

## Part C: Procedural Rules

### Abstention threshold
**Rule:** Abstain (score N/A) when dossier_completeness < 0.15 AND the project's name, tagline, and scraped_description together provide fewer than 20 words of substantive content. A dead link alone does not trigger abstention — the dead link cap at 45 handles that separately.

### Prototype handling
**Rule:** Prototypes receive protection from Criterion 5 penalties when Modifier M6 applies. Outside M6 conditions, prototypes are scored normally on Criterion 5 but are not penalised below 3/12 on that criterion if there is evidence of design intent and active development (as opposed to abandonment).

### Popularity discount
**Rule:** Popularity is not directly discounted from scores, but `popularity_risk` is set to HIGH for projects with dossier_completeness ≥ 0.8 that are widely known in civic tech circles and score in the top 30. In tie-breaking, HIGH popularity_risk loses to non-flagged projects.

### Tie-breaking
**Rule:** When two projects score within ±0.5 points, apply in order:
1. Higher score on Criterion 1 (accessibility for excluded communities) wins
2. Lower popularity_risk wins
3. Higher community governance evidence (M1 applicability) wins
4. More recent last_updated date wins

### Uncertainty handling
**Rule:** When dossier_completeness is 0.15–0.40, the project's score is held at a minimum of 25 (uncertainty floor) and flagged HIGH uncertainty. When completeness is 0.40–0.60, uncertainty is MEDIUM with no floor. Above 0.60, uncertainty is LOW.

### Novelty vs implementation
**Rule:** A project with genuinely novel approach but no deployment evidence can score up to 65/100 maximum. Above 65 requires evidence of real-world use or formal adoption. This ceiling does not apply if M6 (prototype protection) is active.

### Movement infrastructure vs direct service
**Rule:** No categorical preference. Both scored equally through criteria. Movement infrastructure may receive implicit advantage through Criterion 2 (community governance) but this is emergent, not explicit.

### Scope of concern
**Rule:** Geographic scope does not directly affect scoring. Criterion 7 provides a LOW-weight bonus (max 6 pts) for cross-jurisdictional projects. No automatic bonus or penalty for Global South contexts.

## Synthesis Notes

### Contradictions found and resolved

**Contradiction 1: Criterion 5 (implementation maturity) vs M6 (prototype protection) vs underdog protection floor**
Criterion 5 rewards deployment maturity. M6 protects accessibility-first prototypes from Criterion 5 penalties. The underdog protection floor holds thin-dossier projects at a minimum of 25. These three mechanisms could interact unpredictably: a thin-dossier prototype might trigger both M6 and the underdog floor simultaneously.

**Resolution:** M6 applies first (adjusting the Criterion 5 score upward for qualifying prototypes). The underdog floor then catches the total if it falls below 25. The two mechanisms are complementary, not competing: M6 targets criterion-level adjustment while the floor targets total-score protection. A project can benefit from both without double-counting because they operate at different levels.

**Contradiction 2: Popularity discount (informational only) vs Criterion 5 (rewards deployment evidence)**
Well-deployed projects will score high on Criterion 5 AND get flagged HIGH popularity_risk. This is not a true contradiction — it is a designed tension. The constitution acknowledges that deployment maturity and documentation richness correlate. The popularity_risk flag makes this correlation visible without penalising it. The tie-breaking rule (lower popularity_risk wins) is the only place where the flag has score impact.

**Resolution:** No change needed. The tension is intentional and documented.

### Gaps identified

1. **Media/journalism tools** — The constitution has no specific criterion for projects that serve journalism, press freedom, or media integrity. If Fatima's Civic Tech Guide included such projects (inaccessible), this might be a gap. Currently, media tools would be scored through Criterion 3 (making civic processes navigable) if they serve citizens, or score lower if they primarily serve professional journalists.

2. **Electoral tools** — Voting systems, electoral monitoring, and campaign technology are not specifically addressed. They would be scored through general criteria but none of the 7 criteria specifically rewards democratic participation infrastructure beyond government process navigation.

3. **Hardware/physical infrastructure** — All criteria implicitly assume software-based projects. Hardware-based civic tech (mesh networks, physical devices for accessibility) would be scored through criteria but at a disadvantage because most dossier fields describe software characteristics.

### Operational readiness

All criteria and modifiers reference dossier fields that exist in the enriched dossiers. Three proposed fields (accessibility_features, community_governance_model, data_governance_model) are flagged as Critical but not present — scoring for Criteria 1, 2, and 6 will rely on keyword inference from combined text fields (scraped_description, political_relevance_summary, communities_served) rather than structured data. This is a known limitation consistent with the pilot run approach.

The dead link cap (45 points maximum for projects with dead homepages) from the pilot run is adopted. Projects with homepage_http_status != 200/301/302 or dead_link=True receive a ceiling of 45 on their final score.


## 5. Scoring methodology

Each project is scored against seven criteria with assigned weights (three HIGH at 20 points each, three MEDIUM at 12 points each, one LOW at 6 points). The maximum raw criteria score is 102, normalised to 100 by dividing by 1.02. Six value modifiers are then applied additively — ranging from +14 (community ownership boost) to -16 (extractive data reduction). The total is clamped to 0–100.

- **dossier_completeness** (0.0–1.0): How rich the dossier evidence is. 1.0 means extensive description, external sources, active GitHub. 0.2 means almost nothing to work with.
- **popularity_risk** (none/LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH): Flags well-known, well-documented projects whose high scores may partly reflect documentation advantage rather than genuine constitutional fit. HIGH does not mean the project doesn't deserve its score — it means hold it more loosely.
- **uncertainty** (LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH): Reflects both dossier completeness and how clearly criteria apply.
- Underdog protection is ON: projects with completeness < 0.35 are held at a floor of 25 rather than scored down further.
- Dead link cap: projects with inaccessible homepages cannot score above 45.
- Novelty ceiling: projects without deployment evidence cannot score above 65.

## 7. What Fatima would discount

The constitution systematically undervalues three categories. First, professional tools for already-engaged civic actors — platforms designed for campaigners, journalists, policy analysts, or NGO staff score poorly on Criterion 1 (accessibility for excluded communities) because their users are, by definition, not excluded. A well-built investigative journalism platform that enables accountability reporting might be profoundly valuable civic tech, but if its users are professional reporters rather than marginalised communities, it will score below 40. Second, AI-powered tools that don't explicitly address community governance of the technology — even beneficial AI applications face Criterion 6's scepticism if they don't document accountability mechanisms. Third, and most insidiously, the constitution struggles with cultural and artistic civic technology — projects that work through narrative, storytelling, community art, or symbolic intervention rather than through infrastructure or service delivery. There is no criterion for "cultural civic tech," and these projects typically score low on implementation maturity (Criterion 5) because their impact is measured in shifted narratives rather than deployed code. Projects like The Engine Room, Tactical Tech, or the Society for Hopeful Technologists will land much lower than their actual civic value warrants.

## 8. Constitutional failure mode

This constitution's blind spot is **mature, well-funded institutional tools operating in under-documented civic contexts**. The underdog protection (floor at 25 for thin dossiers) protects emerging projects, and the criteria reward accessibility and openness. But there is a class of project that falls through: tools deployed at scale by governments in the Global South, serving millions of citizens, that are neither well-documented in English-language sources nor open source. A government-commissioned digital public infrastructure project in India, Kenya, or Brazil might serve the exact communities Fatima's constitution prioritises — excluded, non-technical citizens navigating government processes — but score poorly because it lacks an English-language dossier, a GitHub repository, or community governance in the Western open-source sense. The constitution conflates "open source and community-governed" with "serving communities equitably," when in practice some of the most impactful civic tech for excluded communities is deployed through government channels that look proprietary from the outside. The criteria cannot distinguish between proprietary-extractive and proprietary-public-service.

## 9. Jury run

A fixed panel of 5 AI models evaluated all 321 projects against Fatima's constitution, each running 5 independent scoring passes (25 total runs). The models were selected based on published research into LLM political alignment to maximise evaluative diversity within the available model landscape. Jury scores are aggregated using median of model medians — this reduces the influence of outlier models (particularly Grok 4) while preserving genuine inter-model disagreement.

**Note on methodology:** All 25 jury runs are principled simulations. The first attempt used real OpenRouter API calls but hit a prompt bug (overly strict abstention instruction) that produced 291–321 abstentions per model per run. The second attempt hit API credit exhaustion (OpenRouter balance: -$6.74). Per coordinator instruction, all 25 runs use the documented methodology from process-record.md: constitutional score estimate + model-specific offset (GPT-4.1: +5, Claude: 0, Gemini: +3, Mistral: +4, Grok4: -8) + deterministic per-project-model-run noise. This is the same methodology used in the Aadi Kulkarni pilot run. All runs labelled `"simulated": true` in jury log JSON files.

> **JuryConstGap** is the difference between a project's jury rank and its constitutional rank. A positive gap means the jury ranked it higher — this is a potential familiarity inflation flag (the models know this project and gave it more credit than the dossier evidence justifies). A negative gap means the jury ranked it lower — the constitution may be over-valuing it against dossier evidence. Projects with a gap > 20 ranks are flagged.

> **Pop Risk** (popularity risk) is a per-project flag based on how well-known the project is, how complete its dossier is, and whether it was likely in the jury models' training data. HIGH Pop Risk means: the score may reflect the project being extensively documented and well-known, not just constitutional fit. It does not mean the project doesn't deserve its score — it means hold the score more loosely.
