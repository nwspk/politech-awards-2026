## About Project Mirror v2

Project Mirror v2 is a synthetic evaluator estimation workflow built for the Newspeak House
Politech Awards 2026. It estimates how individual cohort members might evaluate the 321-project
longlist based on their public record and provided bio. It is a research prototype — not a
claim about anyone's true beliefs.

It operates as three things simultaneously:
- A constitutional ranking system — each evaluator's implicit values are made explicit as a
  written constitution before any scoring takes place
- A synthetic evaluator benchmark — testing whether AI can reliably infer evaluative constitutions
  from public evidence and apply them consistently across 321 projects
- A simulated jury deliberation system — a fixed 5-model panel votes on the top candidates,
  introducing structured disagreement into the process

**This output has not been reviewed or approved by Fatima Sarah Khalid. It does not claim to reconstruct
their true beliefs.**

Methodology and design decisions: PR [#76](https://github.com/nwspk/politech-awards-2026/pull/76)

## Scoring methodology

1. **Criteria scoring** — each of the 7 criteria is scored 0–20 (HIGH weight), 0–12 (MEDIUM), or 0–6 (LOW) based on keyword and field evidence from the enriched dossier. Total possible: 102 points, normalised to 0–100.
2. **Criteria total** — sum of all weighted criterion scores, divided by 1.02 to normalise. This is the raw score before adjustments.
3. **Modifier adjustments** — six modifiers (M1–M6) add or subtract points based on trigger conditions. Range: −16 to +14 per modifier. Applied after criteria normalisation.
4. **Final score** — criteria total + net modifier adjustment, clamped to 0–100. Dead link cap (45) and novelty ceiling (65) applied as hard limits where triggered.
5. **Dossier completeness** (0.0–1.0) — estimated from field coverage in the enriched JSON. Low completeness raises uncertainty; below 0.35 triggers underdog protection floor (25).
6. **Popularity risk** — HIGH for well-known projects with rich dossiers (completeness ≥ 0.8) in the top 30. Their scores may partly reflect documentation advantage rather than constitutional fit. Treat HIGH pop risk scores with a bit more scepticism.
7. **Uncertainty** — reflects both completeness and how clearly criteria apply. HIGH uncertainty means the score is a range, not a point estimate. Projects with completeness 0.15–0.40 get HIGH uncertainty and a floor of 25.

## What Fatima would champion

- **Open-source participatory democracy platforms** — CONSUL Democracy, Decidim, LiquidFeedback all hit the triple filter: accessibility (C1), open source governance (C2), and government legibility (C3). These projects don't just let citizens participate — they give communities governance over the platform itself. CONSUL's foundation model and Decidim's democratic association structure both trigger the M1 community ownership boost.
- **Civic infrastructure that makes government navigable** — Tools like Alaveteli (FOI requests), Your Priorities (citizen consultation), and Polis (collective intelligence for policy) all translate complex government processes into something ordinary people can use without professional mediation. This is the drone-companion-app ethos scaled up.
- **Community-governed alternatives to extractive platforms** — Bonfire and Mastodon score well because they combine open source architecture with genuine community governance. Fairbnb.coop earned the highest modifier score (+16) for combining cooperative ownership with underserved-context design — exactly the kind of structural alternative to extraction that her "open source x intersectionality" stance rewards.
- **Government digital public goods** — mySociety Datasets, CKAN, OpenCRVS: open-source tools that governments adopt to serve citizens, with active community contribution pathways. The sweet spot where open source meets government deployment meets citizen benefit.

## What Fatima would discount

- **Professional tools for already-engaged civic actors** — Platforms designed for campaigners, journalists, policy analysts, or NGO staff score poorly on C1 because their users are, by definition, not excluded. A well-built investigative journalism platform might be profoundly valuable civic tech, but if its users are professional reporters rather than marginalised communities, it lands below 40.
- **AI-powered tools without community governance** — Even beneficial AI applications face C6's scepticism if they don't document accountability mechanisms or community oversight. The constitution can't distinguish between "AI for good" that empowers communities and "AI for good" that concentrates institutional power.
- **Cultural and artistic civic technology** — Projects that work through narrative, storytelling, or community art rather than infrastructure or service delivery. There is no criterion for "cultural civic tech," and these projects score low on C5 because their impact is measured in shifted narratives rather than deployed code. Projects like The Engine Room or Tactical Tech land lower than their civic value warrants.
- **Closed-source government tools** — Even if they serve the exact communities Fatima's constitution prioritises, proprietary government-commissioned tools without open source or community governance will score poorly on C2 and miss M1. The constitution conflates "open and community-governed" with "serving communities equitably."

## Limitations of the constitution

- **Open source as proxy for community benefit:** C2's HIGH weight means projects must be open source to score well, even when some of the most impactful civic tech for excluded communities is deployed through government channels that look proprietary from outside. The constitution can't distinguish proprietary-extractive from proprietary-public-service.
- **Participatory democracy dominance:** The top 5 are all participatory democracy platforms. This over-represents one category relative to Fatima's actual interests, which span government process legibility, developer communities, and open source infrastructure more broadly. The three HIGH-weight criteria create a natural funnel toward this archetype.
- **Keyword-based scoring underweights structural value:** Dossier keyword extraction can't fully capture governance models, accessibility mechanisms, or community ownership structures. Projects whose accessibility value is structural rather than keyword-explicit get underscored — the jury data (when available) consistently promotes these projects.
- **Missing critical dossier fields:** Three fields the constitution needs most — accessibility_features, community_governance_model, data_governance_model — don't exist in the enriched dossiers. Criteria 1, 2, and 6 rely on keyword inference from combined text fields, which is a meaningful loss of precision.
- **Mature institutional tools in under-documented Global South contexts:** The constitutional failure mode. A government digital infrastructure project in India, Kenya, or Brazil might serve the exact communities this constitution prioritises but score poorly because it lacks an English-language dossier, a GitHub repo, or community governance in the Western open-source sense.
- **M2 (surveillance reduce) rarely fires:** Most civic tech projects don't describe surveillance in their dossiers, so this modifier has limited ranking impact despite being the strongest negative modifier (−10–16 points). The dossier evidence simply isn't rich enough to trigger it.
