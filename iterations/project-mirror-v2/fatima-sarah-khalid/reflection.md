# Project Mirror v2 — Reflection
## Evaluator: Fatima Sarah Khalid

---

## What Fatima Would Champion

The projects that rise to the top under this constitution share a specific DNA: they are open-source civic infrastructure tools with genuine community governance, designed to make government processes navigable by ordinary citizens rather than professionals. CONSUL Democracy, Decidim, and LiquidFeedback exemplify this — platforms where communities don't just use the technology but shape its roadmap and governance. The constitution's three HIGH-weight criteria (accessibility for excluded communities, open source + community governance, making government legible) function as a triple filter: a project must hit at least two of these to break into the top 30, and all three to reach the top 10. The strong community ownership modifier (M1, +8–14 points) then separates true community-governed projects from those that are merely open source. Fairbnb.coop scores highest on modifiers precisely because it combines cooperative ownership with underserved-context design. The ideal project from Fatima's constitutional perspective would be something like a community-governed, open-source platform that helps non-technical citizens in under-resourced contexts navigate a specific government process — with documented DEI in its contributor base. That project doesn't fully exist on the longlist, but OpenCRVS, Decidim, and Alaveteli each come close on different axes.

---

## What Fatima Would Discount

The constitution systematically undervalues three categories. First, professional tools for already-engaged civic actors — platforms designed for campaigners, journalists, policy analysts, or NGO staff score poorly on Criterion 1 (accessibility for excluded communities) because their users are, by definition, not excluded. A well-built investigative journalism platform that enables accountability reporting might be profoundly valuable civic tech, but if its users are professional reporters rather than marginalised communities, it will score below 40. Second, AI-powered tools that don't explicitly address community governance of the technology — even beneficial AI applications face Criterion 6's scepticism if they don't document accountability mechanisms. Third, and most insidiously, the constitution struggles with cultural and artistic civic technology — projects that work through narrative, storytelling, community art, or symbolic intervention rather than through infrastructure or service delivery. There is no criterion for "cultural civic tech," and these projects typically score low on implementation maturity (Criterion 5) because their impact is measured in shifted narratives rather than deployed code. Projects like The Engine Room, Tactical Tech, or the Society for Hopeful Technologists will land much lower than their actual civic value warrants.

---

## Constitutional Failure Mode

This constitution's blind spot is **mature, well-funded institutional tools operating in under-documented civic contexts**. The underdog protection (floor at 25 for thin dossiers) protects emerging projects, and the criteria reward accessibility and openness. But there is a class of project that falls through: tools deployed at scale by governments in the Global South, serving millions of citizens, that are neither well-documented in English-language sources nor open source. A government-commissioned digital public infrastructure project in India, Kenya, or Brazil might serve the exact communities Fatima's constitution prioritises — excluded, non-technical citizens navigating government processes — but score poorly because it lacks an English-language dossier, a GitHub repository, or community governance in the Western open-source sense. The constitution conflates "open source and community-governed" with "serving communities equitably," when in practice some of the most impactful civic tech for excluded communities is deployed through government channels that look proprietary from the outside. The criteria cannot distinguish between proprietary-extractive and proprietary-public-service.

## Reaction Questions

> 1. The constitution weights "open source commitment and community governance" at 20 points — equal to accessibility. In your experience curating the Civic Tech Guide, have you encountered genuinely impactful civic tech projects that were closed-source or government-proprietary? Would you want the constitution to see those differently, or is open source a non-negotiable for you?

> 2. Modifier M2 reduces scores for "extractive data practices or surveillance without community consent." But some government digital services necessarily collect personal data (birth registrations, tax filings, health records) to function. Where would you draw the line between essential government data collection and extractive practice — and does the constitution draw it in the right place?

> 3. The constitution's top-ranked projects (CONSUL, Decidim, LiquidFeedback) are all participatory democracy platforms. Your career has been more about making *existing* government processes navigable (drone regulations, civic tech guides) than about creating new participatory structures. Does the ranking over-weight participatory democracy relative to government process legibility?

> 4. Looking at your top-ranked projects: are there any you'd expect to score lower because they're well-known and well-documented rather than genuinely fitting your constitution? And are there obscure projects buried lower in the ranking that you think deserve higher consideration?

> 5. The constitution treats "inclusive developer community" (M5, +3–6 points) as a weak boost. Given your @drupaldiversity work and WCT Rising Star award, is this weight too low? Should a project's internal community inclusivity be weighted as heavily as its external accessibility to excluded communities?
