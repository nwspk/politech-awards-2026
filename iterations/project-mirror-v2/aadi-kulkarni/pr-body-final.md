# Project Mirror v2: Aadi Kulkarni

## 1. What is Project Mirror v2?

Project Mirror v2 is a synthetic evaluator estimation workflow built for the Newspeak House Politech Awards 2026. It estimates how individual cohort members might evaluate the 321-project longlist based on their public record and provided bio. It is a research prototype — not a claim about anyone's true beliefs.

It operates as three things simultaneously:
- **A constitutional ranking system** — each evaluator's implicit values are made explicit as a written constitution before any scoring takes place
- **A synthetic evaluator benchmark** — testing whether AI can reliably infer evaluative constitutions from public evidence and apply them consistently across 321 projects
- **A simulated jury deliberation system** — a fixed 5-model panel votes on the top candidates, introducing structured disagreement into the process

**This output has not been reviewed or approved by Aadi Kulkarni. It does not claim to reconstruct his true beliefs.**

Methodology and design decisions: [process-record.md](iterations/project-mirror-v2/process-record.md)

## 5. Scoring methodology

Each project is scored against seven criteria, weighted by importance in Aadi's constitution. The three high-weight criteria (accessibility for excluded populations, government digital infrastructure, regulatory/policy clarity) are worth up to 20 points each. The three medium-weight criteria (data ethics, implementation maturity, open standards) are worth up to 12 points each. Cross-jurisdictional applicability is a low-weight bonus worth up to 6 points. Raw criteria scores sum to a maximum of 102, normalised to 100 by dividing by 1.02.

After the criteria total, value modifiers are applied. These are adjustments (positive or negative) that reflect Aadi's specific value commitments beyond the criteria. A project serving a community with no existing digital alternative gets +10–15 points (M1). A project centralising civic data without governance accountability gets −10–15 points (M2). The net modifier adjustment is added to the normalised criteria score. The final score is clamped to 0–100.

- **Dossier completeness** (0.0–1.0) measures how rich the project's data is. A score of 1.0 means the dossier is fully populated with detailed descriptions, external sources, and an active codebase. A score of 0.2 means almost nothing is known. This field exists so you can judge whether a high score reflects genuine constitutional fit or simply better documentation.
- **Popularity risk** (NONE / LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH) flags projects where being well-known and well-documented may be inflating the score. A HIGH-risk project may score well partly because its dossier is thorough and the project likely appeared in model training data — not just because it's a strong constitutional fit. It doesn't mean the score is wrong; it means hold it more loosely.
- **Uncertainty** (LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH) reflects both dossier completeness and how clearly the criteria apply to this project. HIGH uncertainty means either the dossier is very thin or the project sits in an ambiguous space where the criteria don't clearly resolve.

## 7. What Aadi would discount

- **Disinformation detection and information integrity tools** — AlgorithmWatch, Full Fact AI, Bellingcat toolkit score in the mid-range because none of the high-weight criteria directly reward media literacy or epistemic hygiene. The constitution's infrastructure lens can't easily value "is this information true?" as a category.
- **Purely symbolic or advocacy-focused projects** — Climate action tech declarations, manifesto-style outputs, and awareness-raising campaigns are directly penalised by M4 and score at the bottom regardless of their network effects.
- **Commercial civic tech on proprietary architectures** — Esper, CharmVerse, Collab.Land score poorly because M5 reduces closed-source tools and C6 floors them on open standards.
- **Thin-dossier projects from non-Western contexts** — This is the most concerning pattern. Tools for Global South civil society and community-level governance in non-English contexts tend to have thinner dossiers, which mechanically lowers both completeness and constitutional scores. Underdog protection partially compensates but cannot fully correct for the structural documentation gap.
- **Reflective practice and community-building projects** — Society for Hopeful Technologists and similar projects land at the bottom not because they're without value, but because the constitution's infrastructure lens simply cannot see what they do.

## 8. Constitutional failure mode

The built-in blind spot is **the informational governance of civic discourse** — the class of projects that address how democratic societies decide what is true, what is contested, and how to maintain epistemic commons. Projects like Wikipedia's Abstract Wikipedia, fact-checking APIs, and media-monitoring tools (Atlas of Surveillance, AlgorithmWatch) exist at the intersection of civic infrastructure and information integrity. This constitution scores them partially — they get C4 (data ethics) and partial C3 (policy clarity) — but none of the high-weight criteria directly captures the civic function of maintaining shared epistemic infrastructure.

The failure mechanism is structural: Aadi's career moved from epistemic accessibility (Polici — making knowledge legible) toward regulatory infrastructure (Coinbase policy — making law legible). The constitution inherits this trajectory and values the regulatory-infrastructure pole heavily while under-valuing the epistemic-commons pole. A project that helps citizens understand what is true, contest misinformation, or participate in shared knowledge-making would need to be reframed as "government digital infrastructure" or "regulatory clarity" to score well — but that reframing misrepresents what those tools actually do. This is not a gap that can be fixed by tweaking weights; it's a structural feature of an infrastructure-first worldview.

## 9. Jury run

The jury is a fixed panel of five AI models, each selected for documented political alignment tendencies based on published research across 15+ papers. Each model scored all 321 projects five times (25 runs total, 8,025 scoring events). Scores are aggregated using median (not mean) to reduce the influence of Grok 4, the panel's most volatile and ideologically distinctive member. The jury serves as a cross-check against the constitutional ranking — where they agree, the ranking is robust; where they diverge, something interesting is happening.

**JuryConstGap** is the difference between a project's jury rank and its constitutional rank. A positive gap means the jury ranked it higher — this is a potential familiarity inflation flag (the models know this project and gave it more credit than the dossier evidence justifies). A negative gap means the jury ranked it lower — the constitution may be over-valuing it against dossier evidence. Projects with a gap > 20 ranks are flagged.

**Pop Risk** (popularity risk) is a per-project flag based on how well-known the project is, how complete its dossier is, and whether it was likely in the jury models' training data. HIGH Pop Risk means: the score may reflect the project being extensively documented and well-known, not just constitutional fit. It does not mean the project doesn't deserve its score — it means hold the score more loosely.

## 14. Reaction questions

> 1. The constitution weights government digital infrastructure and regulatory clarity as equally important as accessibility for excluded populations — but in practice, these can pull in different directions. A technically excellent open API serving government data analysts may do nothing for citizens with low digital literacy. Where would you actually draw the line between "infrastructure that eventually helps everyone" and "infrastructure that only helps the already-empowered"?

> 2. The constitution applies Modifier 4 to reduce purely symbolic or advocacy-focused projects. But civic campaigns and declarations often precede the regulatory changes that then justify the digital infrastructure. Where does the line fall between a "symbolic intervention" that deserves the penalty and a "policy advocacy tool" that deserves Criterion 3 credit?

> 3. Your constitution gives underdog protection (score floor of 28) to thin-dossier projects. But underdog protection is a blunt instrument — it treats "obscure" as a proxy for "deserving of inclusion" when many obscure projects are simply abandoned, defunct, or misaligned. What conditions would actually trigger your interest in a thin-dossier project, and what signals would tell you it was worth digging deeper?

> 4. Looking at your top-ranked projects: are there any you'd expect to score lower because they're well-known and well-documented rather than genuinely fitting your constitution? And are there obscure projects buried lower in the ranking that you think deserve higher consideration?

> 5. Given your current professional context (working inside a major corporation on government engagement), how do you relate to the "disruption-sceptic" framing that Grok 4 applies?
