## Part B: Value Modifiers

### Modifier 1: Human-in-the-loop governance and judgment preservation
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** moderate (+6–10 points)
- **Applies when:** The project explicitly preserves human judgment in consequential decisions — not just nominally but structurally. The system is designed so that human judgment is exercised, not bypassed. AI augments human capacity rather than replacing it. There is a documented mechanism for human review of AI-generated outputs before action. Human actors retain meaningful decision authority.
- **Evidence:** arXiv paper's central finding: the "interactive" condition (human judgment applied) outperforms the "naive" condition (AI output accepted passively). Design philosophy confirmed at Federal Reserve context where stakes of automated financial supervision are systemic.

### Modifier 2: Surveillance, opacity, or concentration of power without accountability
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** moderate to strong (−8–15 points)
- **Applies when:** The project: (a) collects or processes data about individuals or communities without transparent governance; (b) concentrates informational or decision-making power in an opaque institutional or commercial actor; (c) is designed for or primarily used by state or corporate actors to monitor, profile, or control populations without accountability mechanisms; (d) actively reduces human autonomy or community self-determination. The trigger is accountability-free concentration — not state involvement per se.
- **Evidence:** Bio names "attention, autonomy, and community" as the stakes. Federal Reserve context heightens sensitivity to systemic accountability failures.

## Synthesis Notes

### Contradictions found and resolved

**Contradiction 1: Uncertainty handling vs. non-AI criterion scoring**
Criteria 1 (AI safety/alignment) says "absence of safety evidence is a negative signal." But for projects with no AI involvement at all, this criterion should neither reward nor penalise. Without explicit procedural handling, a non-AI civic tool would score near-zero on C1 because there's "no documented safety methodology" — but that would be false reasoning (you can't fault a mapping tool for not having an LLM alignment approach).

*Resolution:* C1 is scored N/A (treated as midpoint 10/20) for projects with no AI involvement. The procedural note on C1 explicitly states this. The uncertainty handling rule applies only to AI projects where safety documentation is relevant. Non-AI projects receive neutral scoring on C1.

**Contradiction 2: NO underdog protection vs. evidence-standard scepticism**
Nicholas Botti is sceptical of naive data-driven approaches. But rejecting underdog protection means systematically disadvantaging under-resourced projects. Is that inconsistent with the complexity/scepticism framing?

*Resolution:* No contradiction. His scepticism of data-driven approaches cuts both ways — he would be equally sceptical of assuming under-documented projects are good. The Federal Reserve context is determinative: thin evidence in a regulatory context is a negative finding. The constitution documents this bias explicitly rather than correcting it silently.

**Contradiction 3: Modifier 2 (reduce surveillance/opacity) and Criterion 2 (reward regulatory infrastructure)**
Some government regulatory tools could score well on Criterion 2 (they help regulators) but also trigger Modifier 2 (they collect data about individuals without fully transparent governance). Example: a financial surveillance tool that monitors banks but also captures private financial data.

*Resolution:* Modifier 2 applies only to accountability-free concentration of power — it explicitly does NOT apply to government regulatory tools that have established accountability mechanisms (legislative oversight, audit rights, public disclosure requirements). The trigger for Modifier 2 is opacity and absence of accountability, not state involvement. A well-governed regulatory data tool does not trigger Modifier 2.

### Gaps identified

**Gap — AI-free projects are disadvantaged by constitution design:** C1 (20 pts) scores at midpoint for non-AI projects. This means the maximum achievable score for a well-designed non-AI project is approximately 90/100 (C1 maxed at 10, not 20). This is a constitution-level bias toward AI-involving projects. It is acknowledged and flows from the evaluator's core focus. Noted here so the ranking agent applies it consistently.

**Gap — No criterion directly addresses financial technology or FinTech:** Despite Nicholas Botti's Fed background, the criteria don't specifically reward FinTech projects. C2 covers regulatory/institutional infrastructure broadly, which includes FinTech that engages with regulators — but a FinTech project that doesn't engage with regulation scores no differently than any other non-regulatory-engagement project. This is probably correct given the stated focus on political technology broadly, not FinTech specifically.
