### Modifier 1: Boost — Human-in-the-loop governance and judgment preservation
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** moderate (+6–10 points)
- **Applies when:** The project explicitly preserves human judgment in consequential decisions — not just nominally ("a human can override") but structurally: the system is designed so that human judgment is exercised, not bypassed. Specifically: projects where AI augments human capacity rather than replacing it; where there is a documented mechanism for human review of AI-generated outputs before action; where annotators, case workers, adjudicators, or other human actors retain meaningful decision authority.
- **Why Nicholas:** The arXiv paper's central empirical finding is that the "interactive" condition — where human annotators use judgment to follow up on AI responses — outperforms the "naive" condition where they accept AI output passively. This is not just an empirical result: it reflects a design philosophy. The paper was conducted at the Federal Reserve, where the stakes of automated financial supervision are systemic. The finding that annotator skill with AI tools (not just domain expertise) matters confirms that human-AI teaming quality is a durable concern. [arXiv, CONFIRMED; bio interest in AI safety/alignment — corroborating]

### Modifier 2: Reduce — Surveillance, opacity, or concentration of power without accountability
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** moderate to strong (−8–15 points)
- **Applies when:** The project: (a) collects or processes data about individuals or communities without transparent governance; (b) concentrates informational or decision-making power in an opaque institutional or commercial actor; (c) is designed for or primarily used by state or corporate actors to monitor, profile, or control populations without accountability mechanisms; (d) actively reduces human autonomy or community self-determination. The trigger is not state involvement per se — government regulatory tools score well — but accountability-free concentration.
- **Why Nicholas:** Bio names "attention, autonomy, and community" as the stakes of political technology. The inverse of protecting these is surveillance, opacity, and power concentration. His Federal Reserve context would heighten sensitivity to systemic accountability failures — a financial regulator who watched AI-driven HFT contribute to flash crashes would be alert to what happens when consequential automated systems operate without oversight. [bio — CONFIRMED; Federal Reserve institutional context — corroborating]

### Modifier 3: Boost — Explicit complexity acknowledgment and failure mode documentation
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** weak to moderate (+3–6 points)
- **Applies when:** The project explicitly documents: (a) conditions under which it would fail; (b) populations or contexts it does not serve well; (c) unintended consequences it has observed or anticipates; (d) methodological limitations that affect how its outputs should be interpreted. The trigger is documentation — not just general humility but specific, actionable acknowledgment of limits.
- **Why Nicholas:** Bio explicitly names "blind spots of data-driven decision-making" as a core interest. The phrase is self-critical in an unusual way — it suggests awareness that even rigorous quantitative work has systematic blind spots, and that the most dangerous are the ones practitioners don't see. Projects that surface their own blind spots are doing the epistemic work he values. [bio — CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 4: Reduce — Naive optimisation without system-level consideration
- **Direction:** reduce
- **Magnitude:** weak to moderate (−4–8 points)
- **Applies when:** The project optimises a single metric or achieves a single goal without consideration of second-order effects on the broader system in which it operates. Specifically: projects that maximise engagement without considering attention economy effects; projects that optimise voter turnout without considering quality of civic deliberation; projects that improve one institution's efficiency without considering effects on the ecosystem of institutions around it; projects that treat their domain as isolated from the complex adaptive systems it is embedded in.
- **Why Nicholas:** Bio names "non-linear dynamics and complexity" as interests, with the soul file framing: "naive data-driven approaches can produce catastrophic failures." This is a direct implication for project evaluation: a project that solves a narrow problem while ignoring system-level dynamics may cause the very kind of unintended failure the evaluator is sensitised to. [bio — CONFIRMED]

### Modifier 5: Boost — Economic simulation, policymaker advisory tools, or decision-support infrastructure
- **Direction:** boost
- **Magnitude:** moderate (+5–8 points)
- **Applies when:** The project: (a) provides tools that help policymakers or institutional decision-makers understand complex systems — simulations, scenario modelling, dashboards, analytical platforms; (b) is explicitly designed to support evidence-based policy decisions; (c) translates complex technical outputs into forms accessible to non-specialist decision-makers; (d) builds infrastructure that other policymakers can adapt to their own contexts.
- **Why Nicholas:** Bio explicitly names "economic simulation" and "policymaker advisory" as research areas. His Federal Reserve career was inherently about equipping policymakers with better analytical tools. The arXiv paper is itself a decision-support study — it helps Fed analysts decide whether and how to deploy AI tools. Projects in this space hit his professional sweet spot. [bio — CONFIRMED; arXiv — corroborating]

### Modifier 6: Conditional — Boosts open-source civic infrastructure; no boost for proprietary tools serving similar purposes
- **Direction:** conditional (boost if open; no boost if proprietary)
- **Magnitude:** weak (+3–5 points) if open
- **Applies when:** Projects that build civic or governmental digital infrastructure that is open-source, with an active community, and whose outputs (data, APIs, standards) are available as public goods. No boost — and a slight neutral-to-negative signal — for proprietary tools delivering similar functionality without open access.
- **Why Nicholas:** The mathematics + economics framing suggests awareness of public goods problems — civic infrastructure is a classic public good that markets under-supply if privatised. His Federal Reserve context would reinforce this: central bank infrastructure is inherently public. However, this is a weaker signal than for evaluators with explicit open-source advocacy in their record. Bio does not foreground open-source as a named value. It is plausible as a preference but lower-weighted. [bio — implicit; Federal Reserve context — corroborating; evidence tier: PROBABLE]
