## 1. WHAT NICHOLAS BOTTI WOULD CHAMPION

This constitution rewards projects that sit at the intersection of AI governance and institutional infrastructure -- tools that help regulators, policymakers, and oversight bodies understand and manage technological risk, while being honest about what they cannot see. The top four projects illustrate the pattern precisely: AlgorithmWatch (87.5) scores highest because it combines regulatory engagement (EU AI Act advocacy) with documented failure modes and explicit AI safety methodology -- hitting C1, C2, and C3 simultaneously at high weight. Polis (87.4) and vTaiwan (84.5) score through a different but equally rewarded pathway: deliberative tools with verified policymaker adoption, where AI augments rather than replaces human judgment. Matrix (86.4) demonstrates a third route: decentralised infrastructure with government-level deployment (French military, NATO) and unusually candid documentation of its own weaknesses (federation complexity, metadata leakage). Further down, PolicyEngine (75.7) exemplifies the constitution's ideal most purely -- open-source microsimulation for tax policy, used by real policy organisations, with published accuracy validation against official calculations. The constitution's six modifiers amplify these patterns: Modifier 1 (human-in-the-loop, up to +10) and Modifier 5 (policymaker advisory, up to +8) together can add 18 points, which is why Cortico (70.9) scores 14 points above its criteria total. Projects that document their own failure modes receive a further Modifier 3 boost (+3-6). The net effect is a constitution that systematically elevates tools designed for institutional sense-making under conditions of acknowledged uncertainty -- and whose documentation is rich enough to prove it.

## 2. WHAT NICHOLAS BOTTI WOULD DISCOUNT

The constitution systematically undervalues three categories of project. First, direct-service civic tools that help citizens but do not engage regulatory or institutional machinery: FixMyStreet (53) processes 32,918 reports per week but scores 22 points below PolicyEngine (75.7), which serves a fraction of that population. The reason is structural -- C2 (20 points, institutional/regulatory infrastructure) rewards tools that serve institutions, and FixMyStreet's citizen-facing simplicity earns almost nothing on C2 or C3. Second, community organising and movement infrastructure without institutional partnerships: meet.coop (30), CoTech (constitutionally ranked 230 despite the jury placing it at 48), and Cobudget (52) all embody cooperative values that C4 nominally rewards, but C4 is only 12 points and cannot compensate for near-zero scores on C1, C2, and C3. The jury-constitution gap on CoTech (+182 places) is one of the largest in the dataset, suggesting the constitution is genuinely failing to capture something the jury models recognised. Third, projects operating in legally contested or informal governance spaces: Anna's Archive (30.3) receives a Modifier 2 penalty for opacity despite serving an access-to-knowledge mission, because the constitution cannot distinguish between "accountability-free" and "operating outside formal accountability structures by necessity." The absence of underdog protection (Part D) compounds all three discounts -- under-resourced projects with thin dossiers lose points on C6 and receive no floor on any criterion. The ranking agent itself flags "popularity risk" on 15 of the top 30 projects, estimating 5-12 points of documentation-driven inflation per project. This is an honest acknowledgment that documentation quality correlates with score more than this constitution intends.

## 3. CONSTITUTIONAL FAILURE MODE

The constitution cannot see the value of prefigurative political technology -- tools whose contribution is demonstrating that a different way of organising is possible, rather than producing measurable institutional outcomes. This blind spot arises because C2 (institutional infrastructure, 20 points) and C6 (implementation maturity, 12 points) together account for 32 of 102 raw criteria points, and both are calibrated to reward documented adoption by existing institutions. A project like meet.coop, Cobudget, or CoTech -- which builds cooperative digital infrastructure as an alternative to institutional power rather than a tool for it -- scores low on C2 because it deliberately avoids regulatory or governmental integration, and low on C6 because its "maturity" is measured in partnership agreements and GitHub stars rather than community resilience. Modifier 5 (policymaker advisory, +5-8 points) further widens the gap by boosting only projects that translate into policymaker workflows. The constitution is designed around the premise that the most important political technology helps existing institutions govern better. This is a defensible position -- but it structurally cannot recognise technology whose political contribution is modelling alternatives to existing institutional arrangements.
