# Reflection — Francesca Galli

## What Francesca would champion

- **Open civic infrastructure for participatory democracy** — Tools like Decidim, CONSUL Democracy, adhocracy+, and Citizen OS that directly enable citizens to participate in governance at the local level. These hit her C1 (digital commons) and C2 (civic engagement) simultaneously. She would see these as the digital equivalent of the bike lanes in her essay — infrastructure that makes democratic conversation physically possible.

- **Data-for-social-good platforms** — Open data tools like mySociety Datasets, CKAN, and Open Data Editor that make public information accessible and usable. Her DataKind volunteer work and analyst identity mean she values rigour in the service of public benefit — not data for its own sake, but data as a tool for accountability.

- **Community-owned and cooperative tech** — Projects like Tech Coops List, PlaceCal, and Open Council Network that are governed by the communities they serve. Her writing consistently values local grounding and community ownership over top-down deployment.

- **European civic tech** — Projects operating in European (especially Italian/UK) democratic contexts. Her lived experience as an Italian in London, active in PD and Newspeak House, means she has the richest context for evaluating these — and Modifier M6 gives a small scoring boost.

- **Creative/interdisciplinary approaches to civic problems** — Tools that use games, design, storytelling, or art to engage people with politics and public life. Her own magpie philosophy and Newspeak House thought experiments show she values methodological creativity.

## What Francesca would discount

- **Proprietary platform-captured tools** — Any project that builds on or depends on a closed platform, extracts user data without redistribution, or concentrates control in corporate hands. Her AI extractivism critique is deep and systemic — this is not a passing preference but a core political conviction.

- **Tools that digitise power without questioning it** — Government technology that automates existing processes without asking whether those processes are fair, inclusive, or accountable. She wants technology to redistribute agency to citizens, not to make bureaucracy faster.

- **Surveillance-adjacent tools** — Projects that increase state monitoring capacity without corresponding accountability mechanisms. Her transparency and epistemic honesty criteria would flag these.

- **Corporate AI tools** — DeepSeek, FarmerChat, and similar projects that serve institutional efficiency rather than citizen empowerment. These represent exactly the salvage accumulation she critiques.

## Constitutional failure mode

The constitution cannot see valuable work that operates outside the civic engagement / digital commons / data-for-social-good triangle. Projects focused on individual empowerment (therapy tools, personal finance, career development), purely technical infrastructure (programming languages, developer tools), or cultural production (art platforms, music tools) get almost no traction from the criteria — even when these projects may genuinely contribute to human flourishing in ways Francesca herself values.

The magpie philosophy says "pursue all that is Good, True, and Beautiful" — but the constitution only measures Good through a civic lens. Beautiful gets 12 points (C5, interdisciplinary approach) and True gets 12 points (C6, transparency), while Civic gets 60 points (C1+C2+C3). The constitution is more focused than the person it models. This is the structural limitation: her interdisciplinary identity is wider than any single evaluative framework can capture, and the 321-project ranking forces choices that her actual worldview might resist.

Additionally, the constitution has a European bias baked in through M6 and through Francesca's lived experience context — projects operating in the Global South or in non-European democratic traditions may be undervalued not because she would deliberately discount them, but because the evidence base for her values is rooted in European civic life.

Does this feel right, or would you say your civic engagement and participatory democracy interests are actually more central than the AI/commons critique?

But your Substack is more about *how* people engage with politics than about specific platforms. Are there projects in the list that you would champion not because they are open infrastructure but because they change how people *think* about civic participation?

3. Your constitution includes underdog protection — holding thin-dossier projects at a floor score rather than penalising them for being obscure. This is inferred from your magpie philosophy (finding unexpected value). But your DataKind/analyst side suggests you also value evidence and rigour. Would you actually protect a project with no evidence, or would you want to see something concrete before giving it credit?

4. Looking at the top 25: are there projects you would expect to score lower because they are well-known and well-documented rather than genuinely fitting your constitution? And are there obscure projects you think deserve higher consideration than they received?

5. The constitution does not have an environmental criterion despite your Warwick Incubator sustainable swimwear project and your essay about AI's physical supply chain costs. Should environmental sustainability be an explicit criterion, or is it genuinely secondary to your civic engagement priorities when evaluating political technology?
