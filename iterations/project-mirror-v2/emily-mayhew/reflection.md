## What Emily Mayhew Would Champion

This constitution is built to find projects that sit at the intersection of three things: operational government infrastructure, cultural/creative sector relevance, and genuine care for the people affected by the technology. The projects that score highest are those that combine open civic infrastructure with direct government deployment and a clear connection to protecting or empowering communities. Open Digital Planning tops the ranking because it is exactly the kind of tool Emily Mayhew has spent her career working alongside — digital infrastructure that local authorities actually use, that is open-source, and that directly shapes how planning decisions affect communities. ODK and OpenCRVS score high for the same structural reason: they are deployed government tools that serve populations who would otherwise be administratively invisible. The creative industries criterion (C3, 20 pts) creates a distinctive second lane — projects like Open Data Editor and mySociety's data tools score well because their work touches content, media, and cultural data, even when they are not arts organisations per se. The modifier stack amplifies this: M1 (government–civil society bridge) and M2 (creative workers) each add 5–10 points to projects that already score well on criteria, creating a compounding effect that rewards the specific kind of bridging work that defines her career arc.

## What Emily Mayhew Would Discount

The constitution systematically under-values three types of work. First, technology-first tools without a human-centred narrative — blockchain projects, decentralised protocols, and cryptographic infrastructure that leads with technical architecture rather than who it helps. Modifier M3 (−5 to −10) actively penalises these, and several of the criteria (C1 government deployment, C2 affected populations, C3 creative industries) simply don't have room for tools whose primary value is technical innovation. Second, projects focused on individual empowerment rather than institutional change — personal productivity tools, individual data rights apps, tools that help one person navigate one system. The constitution is structurally oriented toward infrastructure and institutions, not individual users. Third, projects in domains entirely outside her professional experience — agricultural tech, environmental monitoring, health informatics — that may be genuinely valuable political technology but don't trigger any of the seven criteria or six modifiers. The List (ranked 320) is an example: a project about refugee experience that might seem aligned with her values but that the dossier presents in a way the scoring keywords don't capture.

## Constitutional Failure Mode

This constitution cannot see the value of political technology that operates through cultural, artistic, or symbolic intervention rather than institutional infrastructure. Projects like Democracy Club's video work, community arts technology, satirical or activist media tools, and civic storytelling platforms fall through the gap between Criterion 3 (creative industries — which rewards industry-facing tools and copyright/licensing infrastructure) and Criterion 2 (affected populations — which rewards direct service to vulnerable groups). A project that uses art to shift political discourse, or technology to enable creative resistance, scores low on C1 (no government deployment), low on C5 (often ephemeral by design), and only modestly on C3 (which is calibrated toward the content marketplace and licensing, not cultural production as political action). Emily Mayhew's own X/Twitter bio centres "culture and creativity in society" — but the constitution operationalises this as creative industries policy rather than culture as a political force. This is the most likely point of disagreement between the real person and the synthetic estimate.
