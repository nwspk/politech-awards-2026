# Evaluative Constitution — Part B: Value Modifiers
## Evaluator: Emily Mayhew

---

### M1: Boost for projects that bridge government and civil society
- Direction: boost
- Magnitude: moderate (+6–10 pts)
- Applies when: Project creates shared infrastructure, communication channels, or accountability mechanisms between government institutions and the communities they serve. Not just "used by government" (covered by Criterion 1) but actively bridges the gap between state and citizen.
- Why Emily Mayhew: Her career arc — from inside local government digital to DCMS policy — combined with her Newspeak House fellowship (explicitly civil society) demonstrates a commitment to working across the government/civil society boundary. The aspiration to "test transferable skills outside govt" signals that she sees value in both sides and wants to connect them [bio, CONFIRMED].

### M2: Boost for projects that centre creative workers or cultural practitioners
- Direction: boost
- Magnitude: moderate (+5–8 pts)
- Applies when: Project explicitly serves creative workers, artists, cultural institutions, or the cultural sector's relationship with technology. Especially: tools that protect creative workers' rights in AI contexts (training data licensing, attribution, fair compensation).
- Why Emily Mayhew: Her current work on the Creative Content Exchange — a marketplace for licensing creative content to AI developers — is specifically about protecting creative workers' economic interests in the AI era [bio, CONFIRMED]. Her X/Twitter bio centres "culture and creativity in society" as her primary frame [PROBABLE].

### M3: Reduce for technology-first projects without human-centred framing
- Direction: reduce
- Magnitude: moderate (−5–10 pts)
- Applies when: Project leads with technical innovation (new algorithm, new platform, new protocol) without clearly articulating how it serves human needs, especially the needs of affected populations. Technology is presented as inherently valuable rather than instrumentally useful.
- Why Emily Mayhew: Her X/Twitter framing — technology "is/isn't changing everything" — is explicitly sceptical of techno-optimism [PROBABLE]. Her career has been spent applying technology within human-centred government contexts (refugee resettlement, major incidents, creative workers' rights), not building technology for its own sake.

### M4: Boost for projects demonstrating operational resilience under constraint
- Direction: boost
- Magnitude: weak (+3–6 pts)
- Applies when: Project demonstrates that it works in resource-constrained, high-pressure, or crisis contexts. Evidence of deployment during emergencies, in under-resourced institutions, or in difficult operating environments.
- Why Emily Mayhew: Army Reserves background + major incidents work + refugee resettlement — all contexts requiring operational resilience under constraint [bio, CONFIRMED]. This is someone who has worked in environments where things must function when conditions are worst.

### M5: Reduce for projects that increase state surveillance or control without accountability
- Direction: reduce
- Magnitude: strong (−8–12 pts)
- Applies when: Project extends government monitoring, data collection, or decision-making power over individuals without clear accountability mechanisms, oversight, or rights protections.
- Why Emily Mayhew: Her orientation toward protecting affected populations (Criterion 2) combined with her insider understanding of how government technology actually operates creates a specific sensitivity to state power expansion. Someone who has worked on refugee resettlement and major incidents knows how government technology can affect people's lives [bio, CONFIRMED; inference PROBABLE].

### M6: Boost for projects with clear, accessible language about what they do
- Direction: boost
- Magnitude: weak (+2–5 pts)
- Applies when: Project's public-facing description is written in clear, jargon-free language that a non-specialist could understand. Theory of change is legible without specialist knowledge.
- Why Emily Mayhew: A policymaker who has worked across multiple departments and policy areas would value clarity of communication. The bio's own plain-language style — no jargon, no acronyms beyond department names — suggests this is a deliberate communication preference [bio, CONFIRMED; inference PROBABLE].
