# Reflection — Beacon
## Project Mirror v2 — mirror-reflective output

---

## What Beacon would champion

The projects that score highest under this constitution share a distinctive profile: they are open-source tools built for specific practitioners — journalists, musicians, community organisers, local publishers — who lack free alternatives, and they deliberately route value away from corporate platforms toward the people who actually do the work. The top of Beacon's ranking is populated by projects like Bonfire (federated social networking with community governance), Mastodon (decentralised, anti-extraction social media), Open Council Network (open civic data serving grassroots users), and PlaceCal (community-owned local event infrastructure). What unites them is not just openness but a structural commitment to practitioners over institutions: they are tools that cut out intermediaries rather than serving them. The constitution's three equally-weighted high criteria (C1: free/open access, C2: direct practitioner benefit, C3: anti-extraction) conspire to strongly reward projects that hit all three simultaneously — and the M1 modifier (+8–12 for tools addressing genuine access gaps) pushes them further. A near-perfect project under this constitution would be an MIT-licensed, self-hostable tool that serves a community priced out of existing alternatives, uses decentralised architecture, has community governance, and augments rather than replaces human skills. Beacon's own Gonzo Engineering projects — teeline.online, Soli, Dummy — are the archetype.

---

## What Beacon would discount

The constitution systematically undervalues two broad categories. First, government-facing institutional infrastructure — projects like OpenCRVS, CKAN, or government digital service platforms. These are explicitly deprioritised by C2 (which rewards practitioner benefit over institutional efficiency) and receive no boost from any modifier. A project that makes a government department more efficient scores poorly unless it can demonstrate that citizens or practitioners are the primary beneficiaries rather than the institution itself. This is a deliberate constitutional choice rooted in Beacon's practitioner-first orientation, but it means genuinely transformative public sector infrastructure gets scored as if it were serving incumbents. Second, projects that use corporate platform APIs — even those doing socially valuable work through Facebook, Google, or Twitter integrations — trigger M2 (−8–12) and score low on C3. A brilliant civic engagement tool built on top of WhatsApp or Google Maps would be structurally penalised regardless of its impact on communities. The constitution also underweights projects operating at scale without open-source code: large NGO platforms, well-funded but proprietary civic tech, and anything with a subscription or premium tier lose significant ground on C1 regardless of their accessibility outcomes.

---

## Constitutional failure mode

This constitution cannot see the value of closed-source civic technology that achieves genuine equity outcomes through scale and institutional partnerships rather than through architectural openness. A project like GovTech Singapore or a large-scale municipal digital service — proprietary, government-commissioned, not self-hostable — might dramatically improve access to public services for millions of marginalised citizens, but it would score low on C1 (not open-source), low on C2 (institutional primary user), low on C3 (platform-dependent), and trigger no positive modifiers. The constitution assumes that the route to equitable technology is through open architectures and practitioner empowerment — which is a coherent and defensible position, but one that structurally cannot recognise effective institutional action taken through conventional software procurement. The blind spot is not about Beacon being wrong; it is that the criteria measure *how* a tool is made and governed rather than *what outcomes* it achieves for end users when the two diverge.

## Reaction questions

> 1. The constitution gives equal weight to open-source access (C1), practitioner benefit (C2), and anti-extraction stance (C3) — all at 20 points. In practice, do you think one of these matters more to you than the others? For instance, would you rank a proprietary tool that directly serves excluded practitioners above an open-source tool that primarily serves well-resourced organisations?

> 2. Modifier M4 reduces scores for tools that automate civic judgment or replace human decision-making. Where do you draw the line between augmenting and replacing? For example: would a tool that auto-generates data journalism visualisations from datasets count as augmenting (it helps journalists) or replacing (it does the journalist's work)?

> 3. The constitution has no criterion for government digital infrastructure or policy influence — your work operates outside institutional frameworks rather than through them. But as a Guardian software engineer working at the editorial/tech intersection, do you encounter projects in government or policy spaces that you think deserve more credit than this constitution gives them?

> 4. Looking at your top-ranked projects: are there any you'd expect to score lower because they're well-known and well-documented rather than genuinely fitting your constitution? And are there obscure projects buried lower in the ranking that you think deserve higher consideration?

> 5. The "freak kingdom" framing and Gonzo Engineering identity suggest a preference for the irreverent and countercultural. Does this extend to how you evaluate political technology — would you actively prefer a scrappy, idiosyncratic project over a polished, well-funded one doing similar work, all else being equal? Or is the irreverence more about personal identity than evaluative criteria?
