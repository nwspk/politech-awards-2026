### Evidence summary

**Career arc.** Connor Dunlop's career traces a clear progression from conflict studies and security analysis toward AI governance and technical verification. He studied history and politics at Newcastle University, then completed a master's in conflict studies and human rights at Utrecht University. His early career included analytical roles at the UN Refugee Agency and The Hague Centre for Strategic Studies (where he co-authored a snapshot on space and the Netherlands with Patrick Bolder). He moved into EU public affairs at Dentons Global Advisors in Brussels, where he led work on the EU AI Act and AI Liability Directive. He then joined the Ada Lovelace Institute as European Policy Lead, eventually becoming Acting Head of EU and Global Governance. As of 2025, he has moved to Lucid Computing, a startup building cryptographic verification infrastructure for AI compliance.

**Values and priorities.** The public record reveals a tightly coherent set of commitments. First, enforcement over voluntarism: Dunlop repeatedly argues that voluntary commitments and industry-led codes of practice are insufficient, that "companies will choose to prioritise corporate incentives over safety in the absence of a strong regulatory framework." Second, pre-market assessment: the FDA analogy is central to his work — he argues foundation models pose risks comparable to Class III medical devices and should require pre-market approval with the burden of proof on developers. Third, post-deployment monitoring: he extends the FDA model to argue for ongoing surveillance of AI systems after deployment, including ecosystem-wide reporting and trusted flagging mechanisms. Fourth, meaningful participation: he draws a sharp distinction between genuine participatory governance and "tick-box exercises," advocating for citizen assemblies and patient-representative-style inclusion in AI oversight. Fifth, centralized regulatory capacity: he consistently argues for a dedicated EU AI office with technical expertise, funded by mandatory developer fees.

**The Lucid Computing move.** This is the most significant recent signal. Lucid Computing builds cryptographic proof of compliance for AI systems — TEE-based verification, automated audit trails, AI Passports. This represents a transition from policy advocacy to technical infrastructure for enforcement. The move suggests Dunlop believes the governance gap is not primarily a policy gap but a verification gap: the tools to prove compliance don't yet exist, and building them is the bottleneck.

**Evidence quality.** The record is strong on institutional positions and policy arguments but thin on personal voice. Almost all evidence comes from Dunlop speaking in institutional capacity (Ada Lovelace, Dentons, OECD). The European AI Fund interview is the closest to a personal statement of values. There is no personal blog, Substack, or independent writing outside institutional publications. Lucid Computing has no authored content by Dunlop. LinkedIn is auth-walled. This means the constitution will be built primarily from institutional positions, which tend to be more cautious and consensus-oriented than private views.
