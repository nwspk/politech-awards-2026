## What Connor Dunlop would champion

- **Algorithmic accountability organisations that build enforcement infrastructure** -- AlgorithmWatch (ranked 1st, 92.7) is the constitutional champion because it combines exactly the elements Dunlop has advocated throughout his career: independent oversight of algorithms, cross-jurisdictional European reach, documented policy outcomes including the EU AI Act's mandatory Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment, and post-deployment monitoring. It is the closest real-world analogue to the FDA-style oversight model Dunlop co-authored.

- **Participatory democracy platforms with genuine co-governance** -- Decidim (ranked 2nd, 62.6), LiquidFeedback (ranked 3rd, 62.5), and CONSUL Democracy (ranked 6th, 55.7) all score well because they give communities structural governing power, not advisory roles. Dunlop's insistence that participation must be "deliberative, and not simply consultations or tick-box exercises" maps directly onto platforms where citizens make binding decisions.

- **Transparency and accountability tools that serve institutional governance** -- Alaveteli, mySociety Datasets and APIs, and Open Ownership score well because they build infrastructure that democratic institutions use for oversight. These are tools that make governance enforceable by making information accessible and institutions accountable.

- **Cross-jurisdictional governance infrastructure** -- Projects that work across borders (Ushahidi, Creative Commons, Matrix) score well on C5 because Dunlop's career has been spent on EU-level and international governance, where cross-jurisdictional applicability is not a bonus but a requirement.

- **Supply chain transparency tools** -- Open Supply Hub and OpenSanctions score well because they address the "value chain" dimension that Dunlop emphasises: governance is not just about the end product but about the entire supply chain from production to deployment to monitoring.

## What Connor Dunlop would discount

- **Job boards, directories, and information aggregators** -- WorkInCharities (ranked last, 0.0), Ladder Hub, and similar projects score near zero because they build no governance infrastructure, have no participatory mechanisms, and do not address any lifecycle stage. They are useful services but not political technology in the constitutional sense.

- **Purely voluntary tools without enforcement mechanisms** -- Projects that rely entirely on goodwill adoption with no mandatory compliance path trigger the M2 penalty (-8 to -12). This includes some well-intentioned civic tech projects that Dunlop would respect but not prioritise: if adoption is optional and powerful actors can ignore it, it does not meet his standard.

- **Consumer-facing apps without governance architecture** -- Projects like FarmerChat, Watch Duty, and PostBug are useful tools but have no governance dimension. They solve practical problems without building the institutional or democratic infrastructure Dunlop values.

- **Single-issue advocacy without enforcement pathway** -- Projects that raise awareness or campaign on a single issue but do not build enforcement mechanisms or institutional capacity score poorly on C1 and C3. The constitution reflects Dunlop's view that awareness is necessary but not sufficient.

- **Projects that concentrate power in a single corporate actor** -- The M5 penalty (-10 to -15) applies to projects that reinforce market dominance or create proprietary dependencies without governance safeguards. This reflects Dunlop's persistent concern with information asymmetries and corporate control.

## Constitutional failure mode

The constitution cannot see culturally embedded governance that does not produce legible enforcement infrastructure. Dunlop's framework is built around the EU regulatory model: formal institutions, written rules, compliance verification, enforcement mechanisms. This is excellent at recognising projects that build within or alongside that model. But it systematically undervalues governance that operates through cultural norms, community practices, traditional knowledge systems, or informal accountability networks -- forms of governance that are deeply effective but do not produce the audit trails, compliance certificates, or regulatory interfaces that the constitution rewards.

This is not a minor blind spot. Many of the most consequential governance systems in the world -- indigenous land management, community-based natural resource governance, informal justice systems -- operate without the formal infrastructure that triggers high C1 scores. The constitution would score them poorly not because they lack governance but because they lack the specific form of governance that Dunlop's career has trained him to recognise and value.

The failure mode is compounded by the evidence quality criterion (C6), which rewards academic citations, documented methodologies, and replication materials. Projects rooted in oral traditions or community practice may have profound governance architectures but score zero on C6 because they do not produce the kind of evidence that Western research institutions recognise.

The move to Lucid Computing deepens this bias: cryptographic proof of compliance is the apotheosis of legible enforcement, and it is structurally incapable of seeing governance that does not produce machine-readable evidence.
